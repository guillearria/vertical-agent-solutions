Picture a four-crew survey firm on a Tuesday morning. The crews are in the field and the office manager is on the phone with a lender. Meanwhile, three other calls go to voicemail: a homeowner wants a boundary survey quote before putting up a fence, a title company wants to know if the survey for Friday's closing is done, and a flood insurance agent needs an elevation certificate ordered. Most of these calls are routine, and they follow the same pattern each time. That makes them the calls an AI phone and text agent can price.

**Every figure below is illustrative.** Your fee schedule, call volume, and software will change the math. Use these numbers as a template, not as a quote.

## Layer 1: The agent itself

Most AI receptionist products charge a flat monthly fee for a set number of calls or minutes. Published plans show the range. Rosie starts at $49 a month for 250 minutes. Goodcall's tiers run from $79 to $249 a month depending on the number of unique callers. Smith.ai's AI receptionist plans go from $150 a month for 75 calls to $500 for 300 calls. Prices change often, so check the vendor's own page before you budget.

Take a firm running 2 to 6 crews and getting maybe 300 to 700 calls a month. It will probably land in the **$150 to $500 a month** range (illustrative). Charges for calls over your plan's limit are where bills creep up, so ask each vendor how they count a "call." A title company that calls back three times about one closing may count as three.

## Layer 2: Telephony and texting

Some vendors include phone service in the plan. Others run on a carrier you pay separately. Twilio's published US rates show the scale: about $1.15 a month for a local number and under a penny a minute for incoming calls. At 500 calls averaging three minutes, the raw voice cost is around $13.

Add texting (a lot of "where's my survey?" traffic works better as a text) and the paperwork carriers require before a business can send texts. Budget **$20 to $60 a month** (illustrative). This is the smallest line on the bill.

## Layer 3: Setup

Someone has to load your fee schedule, your service area, your turnaround times, and the exact wording for handoffs. Vendors range from do-it-yourself (no fee, but plan on 15 to 25 hours of your office manager's time) to paid onboarding at roughly **$500 to $1,500 one-time** (illustrative).

The fee schedule needs the most care. If your published price for a standard platted-lot boundary survey has conditions attached (lot size, subdivision vs. metes and bounds, rush fee), write those conditions out plainly. The agent quotes only what you write down.

## Layer 4: Integration with job tracking

Status calls are where the savings are, and they depend on whether the agent can see your job list. There are three common paths:

- **Your job tracking or scheduling software has an open connection (an API) or a connector service.** Expect **$1,000 to $4,000 one-time** for a contractor to wire it up, plus perhaps $50 to $150 a month in connector fees (illustrative). The agent can then say "field work is done, the drawing is in review, expected Thursday."
- **You track jobs in a shared spreadsheet.** Cheaper to connect, but only as accurate as whoever updates it.
- **No connection at all.** The agent takes the order number and texts the office. That still cuts interruptions, but it won't close status calls on its own.

For a mid-size four-crew firm, a realistic middle is about **$450 a month plus $3,000 to start** (illustrative).

## Break-even in survey orders and hours

Suppose your fees run around $350 for a mortgage survey, $400 for an elevation certificate, and $600 for a residential boundary survey (all illustrative). Under those assumptions, **one recovered boundary order a month nearly covers the running cost, and two orders cover it with room to spare.** A homeowner who reaches voicemail often calls the next surveyor on the list, so a few missed calls a week can easily cost one order a month.

Hours count too. Suppose 120 status checks a month each take four minutes of office time, including the interruption. That's about 8 hours. At a loaded office wage of around $28 an hour (illustrative), that's roughly $225 a month. Crew-chief time matters more. Every call that reaches a crew chief on site breaks up field work that bills at a much higher rate. If the agent saves each crew chief even an hour a week, that's worth more than the office savings.

Spread the $3,000 setup over the first year and add it to the $450 a month. Under these assumptions, the agent needs to recover **about two orders and save about 10 office hours a month** to break even in year one. ## The calls the agent never handles

Locating and retracing property lines is the practice of surveying under state licensing law. North Carolina's statute names it explicitly, and every state has a surveying practice act. Elevation certificates likewise must be certified by a professional authorized under state or local law. Write these rules into the agent's instructions word for word:

- **No statements about where a property line is.** That includes "the fence is probably fine" and "the pins are usually at the corners."
- **No comments on encroachments, neighbor disputes, or adverse possession.** The agent takes the caller's name and parcel and hands the call to a licensed surveyor.
- **No quotes outside the published fee schedule.** Rural acreage, metes-and-bounds parcels, unrecorded splits, and anything heavily wooded or remote go to a surveyor for pricing.
- **No interpreting flood zones or elevation results.** The agent can take the order. Questions about what the certificate means for an insurance premium go to a human.

Title companies will push on these lines more than homeowners, and they're used to getting fast answers. [The title agency post](/blog/your-title-agencys-five-objections-to-ai-agents/) explains how they think about automated calls from their side.

## What to measure before any vendor demo

Pull last month's call log and voicemails. Sort each call into four groups: quote within the fee schedule, status check, new order, and red-line call. Count each group. If status checks and in-schedule quotes add up to fewer than 150 calls, the cheapest plan or a better voicemail setup may be enough. If they come to several hundred, take those counts to two or three vendors and ask each one to price your actual volume, including how they count repeat calls. Ask your job tracking software provider whether it offers an API.
