A yard with one or two scales and a small office often has the same person running the phone and the scale house. When a truck is on the scale and the phone rings again with "what are you paying for #2 copper," something gets neglected. This FAQ covers the calls an AI phone agent can answer for you and the ones it should always pass to a person.

## What do the calls at a small yard actually look like?

Pull a week of call logs, or just keep a tally sheet by the phone for a week. At most independent yards the list looks something like this (made-up numbers):

- 45% prices: copper, brass, aluminum cans, and whatever the caller has in the truck bed
- 20% hours and directions
- 15% "do you take…" and prep questions
- 10% container or pickup requests from contractors
- 10% everything else: disputes, odd questions, people you'd rather talk to yourself

The first four groups are routine questions an agent can answer from information you give it. The last group is why you need clear rules for what gets handed off.

## Can the agent read out today's prices?

Yes, as long as it reads them off a board you control. Treat it like the whiteboard in your scale house. Someone updates one sheet or screen each morning (bare bright, #1 copper, #2 copper, insulated wire, clean aluminum, dirty aluminum, cans, light iron), and the agent reads only what's posted there. It shouldn't estimate, round, or guess at a grade it can't find.

Every price comes with the same disclaimer:

> "Today's posted price for #2 copper is $3.10 a pound. Prices can change during the day, and your final price is set at the scale once we see the material."

If the board hasn't been updated by opening time, the agent should say that and offer a callback. Reading yesterday's prices as if they were today's is worse than giving no price at all.

## What about "is my wire clean enough?" questions?

The agent can explain your prep rules clearly and the same way every time:

- **Insulated wire:** what counts as stripped versus insulated, and whether you pay separately for high-recovery wire
- **Aluminum:** clean means no steel screws, plastic, or rubber attached. Anything with those gets dirty-aluminum pricing.
- **Appliances with refrigerant:** your policy on fridges, freezers, and window AC units

The refrigerant question matters. Under EPA's Section 608 rules, the final disposer (often the scrap yard) has to make sure refrigerant was recovered from small appliances. One way to do that is to get a signed statement from the supplier giving the name and address of the person who recovered it and the date. EPA doesn't accept a sticker as proof. So the agent can tell a caller, "Bring the signed recovery statement or we can't take it as-is," and point to whatever alternative your yard offers.

The agent should never decide whether a particular load passes. It explains the rule, and the scale operator decides.

## Can it handle container and pickup requests?

This is where the agent pays off fastest. Contractors (demo crews, HVAC shops, electricians clearing out a job) call for roll-offs or pickups and expect an answer the same day. The agent can collect:

- Company name, contact, and job site address
- Material type (mixed ferrous, clean copper, sheet metal)
- Container size requested and a rough tonnage estimate
- Placement notes: gate codes, where the can can sit, time windows

It can then offer an open slot from your schedule or log the request for your driver to confirm. Hauling pricing and container swaps work a lot like they do at a roll-off company, so [sorting call types before launch](/blog/ai-phone-agents-at-roll-off-dumpster-companies/) is a useful read here.

## Where does the agent have to stop?

Three places, with no exceptions.

**Negotiation.** "My buddy says the yard across town pays 20 cents more" is a pricing call only you can make. The agent says someone will call back and doesn't match, hint, or promise anything.

**Payout and weight disputes.** If someone thinks your scale shorted them or their check is wrong, the agent takes the ticket number and a callback number and stops there. It shouldn't explain or defend the weight.

**Anything touching scrap-theft law.** These laws vary a lot by state. Depending on where you are, they can require a copy of the seller's photo ID, a thumbprint for catalytic converters or AC coils, holding certain items for a set number of days before processing, limits on cash payment, or reporting to police. ReMA (the trade group formerly called ISRI) keeps a compiled list of state statutes, and more than 30 states now restrict catalytic converter purchases specifically.

The agent shouldn't try to walk a caller through any of that. The handoff rule stays simple:

- Any mention of catalytic converters: handoff
- Questions about ID, holds, or "do you need my license": handoff
- Callers who sound suspicious (large amounts of new copper pipe, bundles of utility-grade wire, manhole covers, "no questions asked"): quiet handoff with a note, and no warning to the caller

## Won't a suspicious seller just talk around it?

They might try. A fixed rule works better than the agent's judgment, though: certain words and materials always trigger a handoff, however the caller phrases things. Your people at the scale are still the real checkpoint. The agent's job is to avoid saying anything, like "sure, we buy those, come on down," that looks bad later.

## What should I track once it's live?

Check four numbers each week:

- Calls answered without handoff (you want the share of routine calls growing)
- Price-board freshness: days it went live after opening without an update
- Container requests captured versus confirmed
- Every handoff involving converters or suspicious sellers, reviewed by you

If you're also comparing vendors for this, [the equipment rental yard questions](/blog/11-questions-equipment-rental-yards-ask-before/) hold up well for any yard business.

## Before you pick a vendor

Spend one week writing down every call on a tally sheet in five columns: prices, hours, prep rules, containers, and "needs me." Then put your price board into a single sheet you can update in two minutes each morning. If you can't do that part consistently, fix it before any agent reads from it.
