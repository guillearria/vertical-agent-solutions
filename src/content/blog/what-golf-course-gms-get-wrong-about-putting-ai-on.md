The pro shop phone at a public or semi-private course rings all day, and almost never with anything an assistant pro finds interesting. Tee times. What's the twilight rate. Is it cart-path-only. Did anyone turn in a 7-iron on 14. Can you host 96 players in late September.

The National Golf Foundation put numbers to that in 2025: the average course phone rings 40 to 50 times a day, two-thirds of the calls are about reservations and pricing, and U.S. courses collectively burn more than 6 million phone hours a year, worth north of $100 million in staff wages. Plenty of those callers could have gotten what they needed without picking up the phone.

So the phone is a cost center. What keeps most GMs from doing anything about it is a short list of beliefs. Here they are, with a verdict on each. (If you want the plain-English version of what an AI agent actually is, start with [our explainer](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/).)

## "Our members will be insulted by a robot"

**Verdict: half true, and it depends entirely on who picks up second.**

Members get insulted by four minutes of hold music followed by voicemail at 6:40 on a Friday. They are far less bothered by a fast, competent voice that knows their name is on the account, books their Saturday 8:20, and hands them to the golf shop the moment they ask for something it can't do.

The real insult risk is one specific failure: the agent traps a caller in a loop with no exit. Configure a one-sentence identification ("you've reached the automated line, and I can get you to the shop any time you ask") plus an instant transfer on any request for a person. Do that and the objection mostly evaporates. Skip it and you'll deserve the emails.

## "Our tee sheet software already handles bookings"

**Verdict: false, and it's a category confusion.**

foreUP, Lightspeed, Club Caddie and the rest run your tee sheet and your booking page. None of them answers the phone. The booking engine serves the golfer who is already online and already decided. The phone serves everyone else.

Ian Brigmann, co-founder of Club Caddie, made the sharper version of this point in September 2026: answering a call is not the same as resolving it. An agent that can only talk is voicemail with better manners. The one you want has read access to the live tee sheet and permission to write a booking into it. When you shop vendors, that integration is the entire purchase.

## "Nobody calls anymore, they book online"

**Verdict: flatly wrong, by a wide margin.**

Per the same NGF research, only about 40% of golfers book tee times exclusively or mostly online, against 80% to 90% for flights, hotels and rental cars. Nearly a third of golfers pointed at the course website or booking system as the reason they called instead. The single biggest reason, though, was habit: it's just how they've always done it.

Meanwhile 2025 was another record year, with more than 500 million rounds for the sixth straight year and on-course participation above 29 million players. More golfers, more calls, same one phone.

## "It can't handle frost delays, weather holds, and rain checks"

**Verdict: wrong on status, right on judgment.**

Frost delays exist because ice inside the grass blade makes the plant brittle, and foot and cart traffic ruptures the cells. The USGA notes damaged turf can take more than a month to recover. That call belongs to your superintendent, and nobody sane is automating it.

But the decision and the broadcast are two different jobs. Once someone marks "frost delay, first tee 9:15" or "cart path only" in the morning, the agent can repeat it accurately to all 30 people who call in the next hour, push tee times back, and stop the 8:00 group from driving over for nothing. Rain-check questions split the same way: the agent states the posted policy and collects the golfer's name and round details, and a human decides whether to issue the credit.

## "Outing and wedding inquiries are too custom"

**Verdict: true about pricing, false about the first five minutes.**

Your banquet coordinator is also your events salesperson, which means outing leads sit in voicemail while she runs a rehearsal dinner. An agent can take the date, headcount, shotgun or tee times, format, whether they want lunch and an awards dinner, and who's paying, then put a real appointment on her calendar and email the notes before she's off the floor. Custom pricing, contract terms and course-closure decisions stay with her. Our [wedding venue booking-season checklist](/blog/before-you-let-an-ai-agent-near-your-wedding/) covers the same split on the banquet side.

## Lines to write down before launch

- Membership dues disputes and billing questions
- Anything touching a member's account balance or house charge
- Pace-of-play complaints, discipline and guest-policy violations
- Custom outing and banquet pricing, and any contract signature
- The frost-delay and course-closure calls themselves

## Count the calls for one week

Before you talk to a vendor (GOLF.AI launched a golf-specific concierge agent in March 2026, and smaller voice shops are chasing the same category), have whoever answers tally calls into six buckets: tee times, rates and hours, course conditions, outings and events, lessons, everything else. Note how many rang past four rings.

Say you land at 45 calls a day with 12 unanswered. Those figures are illustrative, but they sit inside NGF's range. If 30 of the 45 fall in the first three buckets, you know what you're buying and roughly what it's worth. Then ask every vendor the one question that separates them: can you write to my tee sheet, or only talk about it?
