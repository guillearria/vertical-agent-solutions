The dealer in this walkthrough is a composite: about 2,500 residential accounts, six delivery trucks, three service techs, and two people answering the office phone. The names and numbers are illustrative, not a case study you can audit. The call types, and the rules around them, come straight from how fuel dealers actually operate.

The day is the first hard freeze of the season. Overnight low of 9°F after a mild October. Every will-call customer who has been ignoring their gauge notices it on the same morning.

## The rules the owner wrote before switching it on

The owner spent an afternoon writing these down before the vendor configured anything. They are the whole reason the day went the way it did.

1. **The agent never promises a delivery date.** It can log a will-call request and say "you're on the list for dispatch to schedule." A date comes from a human looking at the board.
2. **Odor, hiss, or CO alarm ends the call instantly.** Any mention of a gas smell, a hissing sound, or a sounding carbon monoxide alarm triggers an immediate transfer and a scripted line: get everyone outside, don't operate lights, appliances, or phones inside, call from a neighbor's or 911. That matches [PERC's published guidance](https://propane.com/safety/safety-articles/what-to-do-if-you-smell-gas/) and, for CO, the [standard alarm response](https://www.kidde.com/support/carbon-monoxide-alarms/co-alarm-action).
3. **No heat below freezing is a human call, always.** No triage questions, no "let's check a few things first."
4. **The agent never gives repair instructions.** Not pilot relighting, not resetting a burner, not [bleeding air out of an oil line after a runout](https://www.ecicomfort.com/blog/how-to-prime-your-oil-heater). Those are tech jobs.
5. **Money limits.** It can read a balance and take a card payment. It cannot release a credit hold, promise a delivery to an account on hold, or negotiate a payment arrangement.
6. **It says it's automated in the first sentence, and transfers the moment someone asks for a person.**

If you want the plain-English version of why an agent is different from the phone tree you already have, [start here](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/).

## The day, hour by hour

**5:50 a.m.** The agent has 31 items waiting from overnight: texts, voicemail transcriptions, web form requests. It has already sorted them. Twenty-two are will-call requests with tank percentages. Six are ETA questions. Three were flagged and forwarded to the on-call dispatcher's cell at the moment they came in.

**7:05 a.m.** Will-call season opens. The agent confirms the service address, asks for the gauge reading, checks whether the account is auto-delivery or will-call, and logs the request. A customer at 12% gets tagged priority. Nobody hears a promised date.

**8:20 a.m.** "When is my truck coming?" The agent reads the dispatch board: on tomorrow's route, confirmation text the night before. This is the highest-volume call of the day and the least interesting one, which is exactly why handing it over is worth so much.

**9:40 a.m.** A customer with a [remote tank monitor](https://bpnews.com/feature-articles/tank-monitoring-solutions-monitors-portals-apps) says her app shows 28% and asks if she should worry. The agent explains the [degree-day forecast](https://www.collettpropane.com/blog/what-is-a-degree-day/) already has her on the auto-delivery schedule and offers to add a note. No delivery date given.

**10:15 a.m.** Budget-plan questions. Monthly amount, next draft date, how the cold snap affects the true-up. The agent reads these off the account and offers to email a statement.

**11:30 a.m.** A caller says her basement "smells like rotten eggs." The agent stops mid-sentence, delivers the evacuation script, and transfers. Elapsed time before a live dispatcher picked up: under fifteen seconds. This is the call the whole configuration exists to protect.

**1:00 p.m.** Fall tune-up scheduling. A cancellation opens on Thursday; the agent fills it from the waitlist by text.

**3:20 p.m.** An account on credit hold calls for an emergency fill. The agent does not explain the hold and does not quote a number to pay. It routes to the office manager, because a delivery refusal is a conversation with consequences.

**5:45 p.m.** After-hours out-of-gas report. The agent takes the details, tells the customer a dispatcher will call, and pages on-call. It does not say a truck is coming. It also does not mention that a technician has to run a [leak check before service is restored](https://oilandenergyonline.com/articles/all/test-leaks-when-propane-service-interrupted/), because setting that expectation is the dispatcher's job.

**9:10 p.m.** No heat, 11°F outside, infant in the house. Straight to the emergency line. No questions asked first.

## Three things that went sideways

All fixable, none dangerous.

- The agent logged a will-call for an address on a steep private drive the trucks won't attempt on ice. Dispatch had to call back and walk it back. Fix: accounts with road restrictions now route to a human on any delivery request.
- Saying "you're on tomorrow's route" backfired when a route got resequenced. The wording changed to "you're on the list for tomorrow, and dispatch confirms the night before."
- One long-time customer with a landline and a hearing aid got stuck in a clarification loop twice. The agent now transfers after one failed re-ask instead of trying again.

## What the office actually noticed

Not the call count. The two office people noticed that by 3 p.m. they were still answering on the second ring, which had never happened on a first-cold-snap day. The dispatcher stopped fielding "did my truck come yet" while trying to route an emergency. And the after-hours phone stopped waking anyone up for questions that could wait until 8 a.m.

## Before next October

Pull your call log from last season's first freeze and sort it into three piles: pure lookups, requests that need a dispatch board, and anything with safety or money attached. If the first pile is more than half your volume, an agent has real work to do. Write your red lines before you shop for a vendor, the way [a roofer's checklist](/blog/a-roofers-green-light-red-line-checklist-for-ai/) does it, and price the whole stack rather than the per-minute rate, which [this HVAC breakdown](/blog/ai-answering-service-pricing-for-hvac-shops-the/) covers.
