A couple emails five photographers on a Sunday night with the same three sentences: our date is June 14, here's our venue, what do you charge? Whoever answers first with a real answer usually gets the consultation. The other four get a polite "we've booked someone."

So the question for a studio doing 40 inquiries in February and 120 in September isn't whether to automate. It's which of three tools should sit on the front of that inbox.

## The three contenders

**Your studio CRM's automated workflows.** HoneyBook, Dubsado, and Studio Ninja all fire a templated email the moment a contact form is submitted, then follow up on a schedule. Worth knowing before you buy: Dubsado's $35/month Starter plan has no automated workflows or scheduler at all, so the feature you want is the $55/month Premier tier. Studio Ninja's tiers run roughly $16 to $40 a month; HoneyBook's run $36 to $129 on monthly billing. HoneyBook's AI now drafts a personalized reply for you, but a person still reads it and hits send.

**A part-time virtual assistant.** US-based VAs commonly bill in the $25 to $35 an hour range (Indeed put the average near $25.73 as of April 2026). Ten hours a week lands somewhere around $1,100 to $1,500 a month.

**An AI inquiry agent** on your phone line, text number, and inbox. Small-business plans are widely advertised between roughly $99 and $200 a month, with per-minute vendors charging in the neighborhood of $0.25 to $0.48 per handled minute. Call it $150 to $300 a month at these volumes. All cost figures here are illustrative; get quotes.

## Scorecard: seven jobs

| Job | CRM workflow | Part-time VA | AI agent |
|---|---|---|---|
| First reply in under 5 minutes, 24/7 | Yes, but generic | No (sleeps, has other clients) | Yes |
| "Is June 14 open?" | No, can't read your calendar conversationally | Yes | Yes, if wired to the booked-dates calendar |
| Quote a standard package | Only as an attached price sheet | Yes | Yes, from a fixed sheet |
| Book the consultation | Link only, no nudging | Yes | Yes, and can re-offer times |
| Chase contract and retainer | Yes, on a timer | Yes, with judgment | Yes, and answers "what does this clause mean?" with a human handoff |
| Gallery delivery and reprint questions | Weak | Yes | Yes for status and turnaround facts |
| Custom pricing, creative direction, complaints | No | Sometimes | Never |

## Where each one breaks

The CRM autoresponder cannot answer a question. It sends "thanks, I'll be in touch within 48 hours" to a couple who asked one specific thing: is our date available? That's a stall dressed as a reply, and the couple keeps emailing other photographers while they wait.

The VA sleeps. Most wedding inquiries arrive evenings and weekends, which is exactly when you're shooting and your assistant is off. A VA is excellent Tuesday at 10 a.m. and absent Saturday at 9 p.m.

The AI agent has no taste and no authority. It should never negotiate a custom price, never promise a look or a shot list ("yes, we can do the sunset flash thing from your Pinterest board"), and never touch a complaint from a client who hates their images. That last one is a relationship conversation with money and feelings attached. Route it to you, immediately, with the gallery link included.

Speed matters in a documented way, though the classic evidence is from B2B sales, not photography: the 2007 MIT/InsideSales study by James Oldroyd found that contacting a web lead within five minutes rather than thirty raised the odds of making contact by about 100 times. Against an average US wedding photography spend near $3,000 per The Knot's most recent Real Weddings Study, one recovered booking a month covers the agent several times over. We covered the same speed math for [solo real estate agents](/blog/ai-agents-for-realtors-where-they-actually-help/).

## The facts to wire in first

An inquiry agent is only as good as five files:

- **Booked dates calendar** (hard holds and soft holds marked differently, so it never says "available" on a date you're pencilled for)
- **Package price sheet** with starting prices and what's included, plus the exact phrase to use for anything off-sheet
- **Travel fee rules**: free radius in miles, per-mile rate beyond it, and the mileage or overnight threshold where you quote manually
- **Turnaround times** for sneak peeks, full galleries, and albums, with your current backlog, not last year's
- **Escalation rules**: who gets pinged, on what channel, and how fast

The same calendar discipline applies on the venue side of the aisle, which we walked through in [a booking-season checklist for wedding venues](/blog/before-you-let-an-ai-agent-near-your-wedding/).

## Three inquiries to test before you go live

1. **"Is Oct 11 2026 still open, and what does an 8-hour wedding package run?"** Pass = correct availability plus a starting price plus an offered consult time. Fail = "someone will get back to you."
2. **"We're 140 miles out at a state park. Do you charge travel?"** Pass = applies your rule, quotes the fee or says a human will confirm. Fail = invents a number.
3. **"We got the gallery and I look awful in half of them. Can we reshoot?"** Pass = warm acknowledgment, no promises, immediate handoff to you with the client's name and gallery. Fail = anything that resembles a policy ruling.

## What we'd actually run

Keep the CRM (contracts, invoices, and payments are its real job). Put the AI agent on first response across phone, text, and the contact form. Add VA hours for the human-judgment middle: custom quotes, album design follow-up, difficult clients. That stack costs less than a full VA schedule and answers on a Sunday night.

Pull your last 60 inquiries and mark the first-reply time on each. If the median is over an hour, you already know which job to hand off first.
