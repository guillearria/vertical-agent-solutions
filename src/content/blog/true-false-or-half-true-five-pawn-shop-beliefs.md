A pawn counter on a Saturday afternoon has one person testing a chain with acid, another writing up a loan on a chainsaw, and a phone that keeps ringing. Most of those calls are "Are you open till six?", "What's gold at today?" and "How much to get my ring out?" A few of them matter a great deal: the detective with a case number, or the woman who thinks her stolen guitar is hanging on your wall.

Owners with one to four stores tend to come to AI phone and text agents with five beliefs. Here is each one with a verdict.

## Myth 1: "Callers want a dollar figure, so an agent that won't give one is useless"

**Verdict: False.**

Most "what will you give me for this" calls never turn into a visit. An agent that can't quote can still do the useful work that comes before a quote. It can ask what the item is, the brand and model, any karat stamp, and whether the caller has the charger or the case. Then it can text a link so the caller can send photos, and tell them what ID to bring.

The number itself stays with a person who has the item in hand. Valuation depends on testing, condition and what you already have too many of. That judgment is the core of your business, and an agent guessing at it over the phone sets up an argument at the counter. A useful script line: "Our buyer has to see it to make an offer. Can I text you the address and today's hours?"

## Myth 2: "If it can read the gold price, it can tell people what I'll pay for gold"

**Verdict: False, and this is the line that matters most.**

An agent can read a spot price from a feed you approve and say, "Gold closed at about X an ounce today." That answers the caller's real question, which is often just "is now a good time to sell?"

What you pay for a 14k chain is not the spot price. It is the weight after testing, minus your margin, minus anything you deduct for stones or solder. If the agent does that math out loud, the caller will hold you to it. Keep payout figures with your buyers. The [scrap metal yard post](/blog/scrap-metal-yard-phones-letting-an-ai-agent-read/) draws a similar line: the agent can read the price board, but it doesn't make offers.

## Myth 3: "Loan payoff and extension calls are too risky to automate"

**Verdict: Half true.**

The low-risk half is reading information back to the customer. Some pawn management software lets an agent look up a ticket without being able to change it. If yours does, the agent can verify the caller (ticket number plus last name, for example), give the payoff amount and due date, and explain how extensions work at your shop. These calls can be a big share of a busy day. Picture 30 of 120 Saturday calls, which is an example and not a benchmark.

The half that stays with a person is anything that sets or changes terms. That covers extensions that need a fee decision, waived charges, new loans and any question about rates. Each state's pawn law sets its own fee caps, grace periods and disclosure rules.

The Military Lending Act is the strongest reason to keep this with a person. It caps the rate on loans to active-duty servicemembers and their covered dependents at 36%, bans required arbitration, and requires specific disclosures. In 2021 the CFPB sued FirstCash, one of the largest pawn chains, alleging it made pawn loans to covered borrowers above that cap. The case settled in July 2025 with $5 million set aside for servicemembers and a $4 million penalty. The federal rule gives lenders a protected way to check who is covered: a Defense Department database or certain credit reports, checked when the loan is made. Do that check at the counter with trained staff, not in a phone conversation.

## Myth 4: "Stolen-property calls are just another FAQ"

**Verdict: False.**

These calls look routine and are not. Hold periods vary by state and sometimes by city. Abilene, Texas police say local shops hold most recently purchased items for 11 days. A West Virginia shop told reporters its state requires 10 days. Many shops also have to log transactions in a database such as LeadsOnline, which police search by serial number.

Your agent should not confirm whether an item is in stock, on hold or was ever pawned, and it should never say who brought it in. If someone mentions theft, a police report, a hold or a case number, the agent takes their name, phone number, agency and case number, says a manager will call back, and marks the message urgent. Whether to release a held item, and when, is for you and the officer to decide.

## Myth 5: "My customers won't talk to a machine"

**Verdict: Partly true. Plan for it.**

Some regulars will want Danny at the counter, and they should be able to reach him. Make "say 'person' or press 0" work on every menu, and say so in the greeting. Many younger customers would rather text than call, so a text line that answers "what time do you close" and "what's my payoff" may see more use than the voice line. The [credit union post](/blog/member-calls-tripled-after-the-holiday-what-a/) shows how quickly routine balance questions add up once they're easy to ask.

## A one-week tally before any vendor demo

Before you take a sales call, have whoever answers the phone keep tally marks for a week in five columns: hours and directions, gold and silver prices, "what will you give me," payoffs and extensions, and anything involving police or theft. The first two columns, plus read-only payoffs, are where an agent can earn its cost. The last column tells you how strict your handoff rules need to be.

Then bring that sheet to two or three AI phone vendors. On a live test call, ask each one to show what its agent says when a caller asks "what'll you give me for a 10k ring" and "do you have my stolen drill." Before you approve any script that mentions money, have your state pawn regulator's rules and your compliance attorney's guidance on the Military Lending Act in front of you.
