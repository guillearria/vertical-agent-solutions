Most fire and security shops with 5 to 40 people don't lose money on the work. They lose it on the calling. Someone has to work the recurring-service list every month, reach a property manager who never picks up, land a date that fits a tech's route, and then get the report filed before the AHJ's window closes.

That work has a shape a phone agent handles well, as long as you introduce it in the right order and fence it off from anything life-safety.

## Lines you draw before the first vendor demo

Decide these before anyone plays you a slick voice sample. The agent hands off to a human, immediately, whenever a caller mentions:

- An alarm sounding right now, smoke, fire, or anyone evacuating. Straight to a live person, with a hard rule that a call unanswered after two rings rolls to the on-call phone and then to a backup number.
- A panel in trouble or supervisory condition, a device bypassed, a closed control valve, a system on test, or anything the customer calls "down."
- An impairment. NFPA 25 treats a water-based system out of service for 10 hours or more within a 24-hour period as the point where the impairment coordinator evacuates the affected area or sets up an approved fire watch, with notification to the AHJ, the fire department, the monitoring company, the insurance carrier, and the occupants. That is a human phone call, not a booking.
- Anything touching the central station. The agent never places or clears a test hold, never verifies a passcode, never authorizes or cancels dispatch on a monitored account.

Everything else (scheduling, reminders, status updates, quote follow-up) is fair game. The [green-light, red-line checklist written for roofers](/blog/a-roofers-green-light-red-line-checklist-for-ai/) uses the same sorting logic if you want a format to copy.

## The exact words before a transfer

Write these once, test them, and don't let a vendor paraphrase them. Three versions cover most of what comes in after hours.

**Trouble signal, no active alarm.** "I'm hearing a trouble condition, not an alarm. I'm going to connect you with our on-call technician now. Before I do, please don't silence or reset the panel. Can I get the building address and the zone or device number showing on the display?" Then transfer, and log the panel make and zone whether or not the transfer connects.

**Water-flow alarm.** "A water-flow signal means water may be moving through the sprinkler system. I'm not able to advise you on that, so I'm connecting you with our on-call technician right now. If your property has a fire safety plan, follow it. If you are unsure whether there is a fire, treat it as a fire and call 911." Transfer without asking anything else.

**Sprinkler leak, or a valve being closed.** "Understood. Closing a valve puts the system into an impairment, and that has notification steps attached to it, so I'm handing you to our technician now rather than booking a repair visit. Please stay on the line."

Notice what none of them do: reassure, diagnose, or promise an arrival time.

## Weeks 1 to 4: outbound calls against the frequency calendar

Start outbound, because outbound is low risk. Nobody is on the line with an emergency, and you are calling your own contracted customers about work they are already due for.

Point the agent at one segment first. Annual fire extinguisher maintenance is a good opener: NFPA 10 requires annual maintenance by certified extinguisher personnel, the visit is short, and the conversation is simple.

Then expand along the frequencies that actually drive your schedule. On the sprinkler side, NFPA 25 puts water-flow alarm device testing and control valve supervisory device inspection on a quarterly cycle, with the main drain test at each water supply riser at least annually. On the alarm side, NFPA 72 calls for annual functional testing of most initiating devices, semiannual visual inspection, and smoke detector sensitivity testing within a year of installation and every alternate year after. The quarterly items are where shops quietly under-bill, because nobody has time to call four times a year for a short visit.

Then there's the deadline behind the deadline. Many AHJs take inspection reports through a third-party portal such as The Compliance Engine, and a common configuration is 30 calendar days to upload a clean report, 14 days when the report lists deficiencies, with a late fee after that. Book a January-due inspection in late January and your filing window is already half gone. The agent should schedule against the filing deadline, not the anniversary date.

One legal item you cannot skip. On February 8, 2024, the FCC ruled that an AI-generated voice counts as an "artificial or prerecorded voice" under the TCPA, which pulls these calls into the consent rules. Inspection reminders to a customer under contract sit on much safer ground than upsell calls, but run your actual list past your attorney. Since April 2025 you also have to accept a revocation made by any reasonable method, including words like "stop" and "revoke," and process it within 10 business days.

## Weeks 5 to 8: an after-hours line that only sorts

Now open inbound, but only outside business hours, and only with an on-call human reachable in one hop.

Sorting is the entire purpose. "My panel is beeping" splits two ways: a trouble condition with no active alarm (run the script, capture panel make and zone, book the morning slot) versus anything active (transfer now). A burglar-alarm customer asking why police showed up, or how to stop the fines, gets a plain explanation and a callback slot. Those fines are real money to them: Philadelphia charges $75 for each false alarm starting with the third in a registration year.

If you're weighing this against an answering service or paying a tech a night stipend, the [towing company breakdown of those three options](/blog/the-overnight-phone-at-a-6-truck-towing-company/) prices out the tradeoff.

## Weeks 9 to 12: renewals and the accounts that ignored you twice

Last, hand over monitoring-contract renewals, expiring agreements, and past-due inspection follow-up where the first two attempts went nowhere. This stage protects recurring revenue most directly, and it comes last because it requires the agent to already be reliable on account facts.

## What it has to read from your inspection software

From Inspect Point, ServiceTrade, BuildOps, or whatever you run, give it read access to:

- Site list with addresses, contacts, and building access notes
- Device and system inventory by site
- Last service date by frequency band (quarterly, semiannual, annual, five-year), next due date, and the report filing deadline
- Open deficiencies and the status of any quote attached to them
- Contract type, renewal date, and monitoring account status
- Consent and do-not-call flags

Give it write access to exactly one thing at first: creating an appointment request a dispatcher approves.

## Two weeks of testing before a customer hears it

Week one, nobody outside the building. Write 25 scripted scenarios including at least six escalations: sounding alarm, water-flow signal, closed valve, "we're on fire watch," bypassed zone, someone claiming to be the fire marshal. Have office staff and techs call in and play them. A missed escalation is a full stop, not a tuning note.

Week two, 40 to 50 real outbound calls to long-tenured, low-drama accounts. Someone listens live to the first 15, then reads every transcript daily. Track four numbers: bookings per 10 calls, wrong-site or wrong-date bookings (target zero), escalation accuracy, and how often customers asked for a person.

## One export, sorted by frequency band

Pull every site with an inspection due in the next 90 days that has no appointment on the schedule. Count the quarterly rows separately, since those repeat four times a year, then estimate the hours it would take a person to call all of them. That number is the real size of the problem, and it tells you whether stage one is worth starting at all.
