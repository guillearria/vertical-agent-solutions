A small commercial brokerage might have 40 active listings, three phone numbers on yard signs and a steady stream of LoopNet and Crexi inquiries landing in email at 9 p.m. Most of those inquiries ask the same few things. A few of them are the start of a real deal. The questions below come up when a firm with 2 to 15 brokers thinks about letting an AI agent pick up the phone, answer texts and reply to email for its listings. Every number here is illustrative, not a benchmark.

## How is this different from what a residential agent or a property manager does?

A solo residential agent is mostly racing the clock on buyer leads, which the post on [AI follow-up for solo real estate agents](/blog/ai-agents-for-realtors-where-they-actually-help/) covers. A commercial property manager is fielding calls from tenants who already have a lease: HVAC failures, after-hours access, emergencies. That's the subject of the post on [escalation rules for commercial property managers](/blog/ai-answering-service-escalation-rules-for/).

A listing brokerage sits in a different spot. The caller usually isn't a client yet. They want facts about a specific property, and the brokerage owes duties to the landlord or seller it represents. So the agent's job is narrow: share what's already public, qualify the inquirer, protect confidential material, and get a broker on the line when the conversation turns into a negotiation.

## What can it answer on its own?

Anything already printed on the listing:

- Posted asking rate or price, and whether it's quoted NNN, modified gross or full service, exactly as the flyer says
- Available square footage, suite numbers, ceiling heights, dock doors, parking ratios
- Zoning designation as listed, with a caveat that the inquirer should confirm their use with the municipality
- Availability date and whether the space is still on the market

The rule that matters is that the agent reads from the listing sheet the broker approved and adds nothing to it. If the flyer says $18.50/SF NNN, the agent says $18.50/SF NNN. It does not guess at CAM charges, hint that the landlord is flexible, or tell a caller a medical use "should be fine" in a zoning district.

## Can it qualify tenants and buyers?

Yes, and for most firms this is where the time savings show up. A good intake covers:

- Who they are (a user, an investor, or a tenant rep broker calling for a client)
- Size range and intended use
- Timing (when the current lease expires, the target move-in date)
- Budget or price range, and for buyers, how they plan to finance
- Whether they already have representation

That last question changes how the broker handles the follow-up, so the agent should ask it every time. As an illustration, if a firm gets 120 listing inquiries a month and 80 are tire-kickers, a broker who opens the morning to 40 tagged, summarized prospects has saved real hours.

## Can it send the offering memorandum?

Only after the confidentiality agreement is signed. Crexi's help center describes letting the listing side attach a confidentiality or non-disclosure agreement to an offering memorandum or due diligence materials, and most brokerages use some kind of CA gate whatever platform they're on. The agent can send the CA link, check that it came back signed, and then release the OM. If the CA isn't signed, it keeps the OM. If the inquirer wants to strike a clause in the CA, that goes to a broker, because editing a legal document isn't an intake task.

## Can it book tours?

Yes, against the listing broker's real calendar, with whatever rules the firm sets. For example: tours only on weekdays, a 30-minute buffer between showings, and a broker confirmation required before any tour of an occupied building. The agent sends the address, parking and access instructions, and a reminder the day before.

## What has to go to a licensed broker?

These are hard handoffs, meaning the agent stops and transfers, with no attempt to cover the gap:

- **Rent or price negotiation.** "Would they take $16?" is the cue to transfer.
- **Lease terms and concessions.** Free rent, TI allowances, term length, renewal options, personal guarantees.
- **Agency and dual-agency disclosures.** Who the brokerage represents and what that means for the caller is a licensee's explanation to give. The rules vary a lot by state. Colorado and Florida, for instance, steer brokers toward designated agency or transaction brokerage instead of traditional dual agency.
- **Anything your state reserves for licensees.** State rules for unlicensed assistants are a reasonable proxy. Arizona's guidance says an unlicensed assistant may not give advice, pricing or opinions of value, or negotiate with anyone about a property. New Mexico bars them from discussing or negotiating offers. California restricts discussing the terms and conditions of a possible sale.

The handoff should be warm: "That's a question for Dana, the listing broker. I can connect you now or have her call you within the hour." Then the broker gets a summary, so the caller doesn't have to repeat everything.

## Who is responsible if the agent says the wrong thing?

Assume the brokerage is. The search for this post turned up no commission rule written specifically for AI agents. Supervision rules for human staff point the same way, though: Illinois makes a supervising licensee responsible for an unlicensed assistant's acts or omissions, and North Carolina warns that licensees can be disciplined when unlicensed employees do licensed work. A careful firm treats the agent like a new unlicensed assistant who reads from a script and never improvises.

## What does a sensible setup look like?

As one example, a firm might start with email and web-form inquiries from LoopNet and Crexi only, keep sign calls going to brokers for the first month, and read every transcript each Friday. Phone answering gets added once the scripts hold up. Look for vendors that can pull answers only from your approved listing data, gate documents behind a signed CA, and hand calls to a named broker in real time.

## Before you sign up with anyone

Pull your state's rules on unlicensed assistants (your state real estate commission publishes them) and mark every task on your intake script as allowed or licensee-only. Take that marked-up list to your managing broker or a real estate attorney. Then ask each vendor to show you, on a test call, exactly what their agent does when a caller asks, "What's the lowest the landlord will go?"
