December is a strange month for a massage studio. The phone rings more than usual, and many callers aren't booking for themselves. They want a $120 gift card for a sister in Denver, or they're trying to use last year's card before their deductible-season knee surgery. Meanwhile the therapists are fully booked and nobody is free to answer.

This walkthrough follows a composite studio we'll call Juniper Street Massage through one busy week before Christmas, with an AI phone and text agent answering the line. The studio isn't real. It's assembled from how small studios commonly run. **Every number below is illustrative**, picked to show proportions, not to forecast your results.

## The studio and its rules

Juniper Street has six therapists. Two do prenatal work, three do deep tissue and sports massage, one does lymphatic drainage, and everyone does Swedish. Two therapists are men and four are women. The owner, Dana, still takes a few sessions a week and handles the business side between them.

Before December, Dana wrote the agent's rulebook on two pages:

- Book only into the live calendar, and only with therapists trained in the requested modality.
- Ask about therapist gender preference on every new-client booking, and never guess.
- Late cancellations (under 24 hours) are charged 50% unless the client is sick. The agent explains the fee and logs the cancellation. Dana decides on waivers.
- Health disclosures, pregnancy questions, and anything that feels off go to Dana, with a summary.

One setup detail matters more than it looks. AI receptionist products connect to booking software like Vagaro or Mindbody in different ways. Some book directly into the live calendar. Others just text the caller a booking link. Dana tested hers with ten fake bookings before December to confirm it was writing real appointments.

## Monday: the matching problem

Monday brought about 45 calls and texts. Most were straightforward: "Do you have a 90-minute deep tissue Thursday after 5?" The agent checked which therapists do deep tissue, found an opening with Marcus, and asked the standard question: did the caller have a preference for a male or female therapist? She preferred a woman. The agent offered Priya at 6:30 instead, and she took it.

A human receptionist does this all day. The difference is the agent doesn't skip the question at 4:55 p.m. when the line is backed up. It's like a bartender who checks ID every time, not just when the place is slow.

## Tuesday: gift cards, at volume

Gift card requests peaked Tuesday, about 30 of them. The agent didn't take card numbers by voice. It texted each buyer a secure payment link for an e-gift card, then confirmed the recipient's name and the delivery date (several people wanted cards emailed on December 24th).

Two callers asked whether the cards expire. Dana had already given the agent the answer: Juniper Street cards don't. That also clears the federal CARD Act, which bars most retail gift cards from expiring within five years of issue or the last time money was loaded onto them. Some states add stricter rules on top, so check yours before you write the script.

## Wednesday: the first health handoff

A caller booked a deep tissue session and mentioned she'd had shoulder surgery six weeks earlier. The agent didn't decide whether that was fine. It held the slot and told her the studio would call back before confirming. Then it sent Dana a text: client name, procedure, timing, requested modality.

Dana called her that afternoon, moved her to a therapist with post-surgical experience, and asked for a note from her surgeon. That's a judgment call about a body, and it belongs to a licensed person. The same rule covered another caller who mentioned a blood clot history.

## Thursday: pregnancy and an uncomfortable call

A woman 10 weeks pregnant asked for a prenatal massage. Many practitioners prefer to wait until after the first trimester as a precaution, and studios set their own policies. Instead of explaining that policy itself, the agent took her details and flagged the call for Dana. Dana called back, explained the studio's approach, and booked her for January.

Thursday evening a man asked whether therapists offered "extra services" and whether draping was optional. Associated Bodywork & Massage Professionals (ABMP) and other trade sources treat questions like these as classic warning signs. The agent did what Dana had told it to: it said the studio provides only professional, therapeutic massage, ended the call, and logged the number. It didn't argue or attempt to "screen" him further. Dana saw the log and blocked the number.

## Friday and Saturday: cancellations under pressure

Holiday schedules collapse late in the week. Friday saw nine cancellations, and four of them were inside the 24-hour window. The agent read back the policy, confirmed the fee, and logged each one. One caller said she had the flu. The agent noted it and told her the owner reviews sick cancellations. Dana waived that fee Monday morning.

Each freed slot went out by text to the waitlist, and seven of the nine refilled. That refill step is where a studio actually earns money back, more than the fee itself.

## The week in rough numbers (illustrative)

- About 260 calls and texts handled
- About 110 sessions booked or rescheduled
- 30-plus gift cards sold, a dozen redeemed
- 9 handoffs to Dana: 5 health disclosures, 2 pregnancy questions, 1 inappropriate caller, 1 fee dispute

Nine handoffs out of 260 sounds small, but each one is a call Dana *should* have taken. If yours run much higher, your rulebook is probably too vague.

## Pull last December's call log

Before you talk to any vendor, export last December's calls or texts, or tally a week by hand, into three columns: bookings, gift cards, and anything that needed your judgment. Then write your two-page rulebook from that third column. That list tells you which calls the agent must hand to you and what it can safely handle alone.
