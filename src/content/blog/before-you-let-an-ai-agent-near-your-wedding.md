Booking season at an independent venue looks like this: forty inquiries in a week, half of them asking whether one specific Saturday is open, a third of the tours no-showing, and four signed contracts sitting unpaid because nobody had time to chase the deposit link.

An AI agent — software that reads an inquiry, checks your calendar, replies, and books the tour without a human touching it — can absorb a big chunk of that. If you want the difference between an agent and a website chatbot, [we covered that here](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/). What follows is the sorting work: what to hand over, what to keep, and what to verify before you turn it on.

## Green light: hand these over

- **"Is June 14, 2027 open?"** The single most common inquiry, and the most mechanical. The agent reads your booking calendar and answers in seconds. Speed matters here in a way it doesn't in most businesses — the [2007 MIT/InsideSales lead response study](https://www.leadresponsemanagement.org/lrm_study/) found the odds of qualifying a web lead dropped 21× between a 5-minute and a 30-minute reply. That was B2B software leads, not couples, so don't treat it as a wedding statistic. But a couple emailing five barns on a Sunday night is not waiting until Tuesday.
- **Quoting published packages, minimums, and peak/off-peak pricing.** Anything already printed on your rate sheet. The agent sends the same PDF you would.
- **Booking tours and reminding people about them.** Slot the appointment, send the address and parking notes, text a reminder 24 hours out, offer a reschedule link instead of a no-show.
- **Sending the contract and deposit link.** Generating the paperwork from a template and delivering it is clerical work.
- **Chasing unsigned paperwork and unpaid deposits.** Three polite nudges over ten days, then a flag to you. This is the task most owners quietly hate and most agents do well.
- **Post-event review requests and vendor referral lists.** Sent a week out, when you've moved on to the next weekend.
- **Basic logistics FAQs.** Guest capacity, ADA access, load-in times, what's included, whether the ceremony lawn has a rain plan (state the published policy, don't improvise one).

## Red line: these stay with a person

- **Custom pricing.** Discounts, off-menu Fridays, "we're a nonprofit," bundled hours. Every one of these is a judgment call about your margin.
- **Vendor and insurance exceptions.** Outside caterer, unlisted bartender, a DJ whose certificate of insurance doesn't name you as additional insured. An agent that says "that should be fine" has created an expectation you may have to break.
- **Anything on event day.** A late caterer, a broken AC, a wedding party locked out. Day-of is a phone-and-legs problem.
- **A distressed couple.** Cancellations, postponements, deaths in the family, breakups. If sentiment reads as upset, the agent's only job is to stop talking and get you.
- **A date conflict.** Two parties circling the same Saturday is a revenue decision, not a lookup.
- **Anything a lawyer would care about.** Contract interpretation, refund disputes, damage claims.

## Pre-launch checks: do these first

1. **Name one calendar of record.** One system — your venue platform (Tripleseat, Perfect Venue, and Event Temple are common ones) or one shared calendar. If availability lives partly in a spreadsheet and partly in your head, stop here and fix that first.
2. **Kill the tentative-hold ambiguity.** Decide what a "hold" means, how long it lasts, and whether the agent can see it. Most double-booking horror stories start with an undocumented courtesy hold, not with software. (The mechanics are the same ones we walked through for [salon booking myths](/blog/no-it-wont-double-book-you-the-myths-salons/).)
3. **Make the calendar the only writer.** The agent should book into your system, not keep a side list. One record, one truth.
4. **Set a confirmation rule in writing.** A date is booked when the signed contract and the deposit both land — not when someone says yes. Electronic signatures are fully binding under the federal [ESIGN Act](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/public_statements/prepared-statement-federal-trade-commission-esign/esigntest.pdf), provided the signer consented to sign electronically and you retain the record.
5. **Handle opt-outs properly.** Since April 11, 2025, FCC rules require you to honor a texting opt-out sent in any reasonable way — "stop," "cancel," an email, a phone call — within ten business days for marketing messages. Your agent must recognize all of those, not just the word STOP.
6. **Write the escalation triggers.** Refund, cancel, complaint, lawyer, allergy, emergency, plus any wording that reads distressed. Those go straight to a human, day or night.
7. **Test with your own weird cases.** Ask it about a date you're holding, a package you discontinued, and a Sunday in February. Wrong answers here are cheap; wrong answers to a real couple are not.

## Sentences the agent must never send

"I can hold that date for you." · "We can probably work with that budget." · "That vendor should be fine." · "We'll waive the fee." · "You're booked!" (before signature and deposit) · "We've never had a problem with weather on that lawn."

Put those in writing when you configure it. Every one of them is a promise you'd have to either honor or retract.

## Try it on one inquiry type

Don't launch an agent across your whole inbox in April. Pick the date-availability question only — the one that arrives fifty times a month and has one correct answer — and let the agent handle just that for two weeks while you read every reply it sends. If it never invents an opening, add tour booking. Then deposit chasing. Venues that run guest rooms alongside events may find the [after-hours phone question](/blog/who-answers-your-b-bs-phone-at-2-a-m-three-options/) worth sorting out at the same time.

The couples you lose in booking season are rarely the ones who toured and picked someone else. They're the ones who emailed on a Sunday and heard back on Wednesday.
