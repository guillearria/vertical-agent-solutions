Every pool service owner knows the call pattern that follows three days over 100 degrees: the phone doesn't ring, it doesn't stop. To show what an AI phone and text agent can realistically do with that flood, here's a composite Monday built from patterns common at residential pool companies. The company: 6 trucks, roughly 350 weekly maintenance accounts, plus a steady stream of repair work. It's illustrative, not a real business, but every call type in it is one pool companies actually field.

## The rules the owner set before turning it on

Before the AI agent ever answered a call, the owner and office manager wrote down three hard lines. Cross any of them, and the call goes to a person, no matter how busy the phones are:

- **Anything touching swimmer safety.** A caller asking whether it's safe to swim right now, reporting a suction or entrapment concern, or describing a child near an open pool with unclear water clarity gets a human on the line immediately, not a callback.
- **Chemical dosing advice.** The agent can explain what a reading generally means and can schedule a tech, but it never tells a caller how much acid, chlorine, or shock to add. This isn't overcaution: the CDC has tracked over 13,000 emergency-room visits a year tied to pool chemical injuries, and mixing chemicals incorrectly is a known way people get hurt.
- **Repair quotes over $500.** The agent can gather symptoms (won't start, tripping breaker, grinding noise) and get a tech on the schedule to diagnose it, but any price above that threshold goes to a human to confirm before it's quoted.

Everything else, the agent was cleared to handle end to end: skip-day and route questions, rescheduling, "my cleaner isn't running" troubleshooting, and non-urgent billing questions.

## The Monday, hour by hour

**7:10 a.m.** First call of the day: a homeowner whose pool has gone green overnight. The agent asks the panic-defusing questions first (is anyone about to swim, is the pump running) and confirms it's a maintenance issue, not a safety one. It books a same-day shock-and-brush visit and texts a "don't swim until it clears" note. No human touched it.

**8:45 a.m.** A caller reports a strong chlorine smell and slight throat irritation near the pool equipment pad. This trips the safety rule immediately. The agent tells the caller to stay away from the pad and ventilate the area, then routes the call to the on-call manager within seconds instead of finishing the intake itself.

**9:30 a.m. to noon.** This is the bulk of the morning: 22 calls and texts about skip days, route timing ("will you still come today, it's so hot"), and "my automatic cleaner stopped moving." The agent handles all of it, pulling each account's schedule and giving a straight answer, or walking through the two or three things that make a cleaner stall (stuck wheel, clogged bag, kinked hose) before deciding whether it needs a tech visit.

**12:15 p.m.** A caller wants to know exactly how much liquid chlorine to add because their test strip reads low. The agent explains what a low reading generally means, offers to send a tech out same-day or next-day, and declines to give a dosing amount. It's polite about it and doesn't pretend it can't help, it just moves the conversation to scheduling instead.

**1:40 p.m.** A pump has failed completely at a property with a repair history on file. The agent books a diagnostic visit, and when the caller pushes for a price over text, it gives a realistic range but flags "final quote confirmed by a technician" rather than a number, since anything past $500 needs a human sign-off anyway.

**2:50 p.m.** A billing dispute: a customer says they were charged for a visit during a week they'd requested a skip. The agent finds the skip request in the system, confirms it was logged, and instead of arguing or crediting the account itself, hands the whole thing to the office manager with the timeline already assembled. Money disputes go to a person, but the agent still did the legwork.

**4:15 p.m. to close.** A second wave of green-pool calls comes in as people get home from work and check their backyards. Same pattern as the morning: triage for safety first, then book. By 6 p.m. the agent has handled 61 calls and texts, escalated 9 (5 for chemical questions that needed a tech visit anyway, 2 safety-related, 2 billing disputes), and every escalation included a summary so the human picking it up wasn't starting cold.

## What changes in month two

After the first month, most owners running a setup like this make three adjustments. First, they tighten the skip-day language, because early on the agent was too literal about "next week" versus "this coming week" and it caused a few missed visits. Second, they add a short recorded or texted explainer for the most common cleaner problems, since roughly a third of "my cleaner isn't working" calls turned out to be the same stuck-wheel issue. Third, they lower the repair-quote threshold that requires human sign-off, not because the agent got a price wrong, but because customers wanted a person's voice on anything over a few hundred dollars, regardless of accuracy.

None of that is a failure of the system. It's the normal process of finding where your customers actually want a human, versus where they just want an answer.

## Where to start

If you run a route-based service business and want to see whether this fits, don't start with the whole phone line. Pick one call type you already know eats staff time (skip-day questions are a common first pick) and route only that one through an AI agent for a few weeks before expanding.
