The dealership below is a composite, and every number in it is illustrative. The call types are not. If you run parts and service at an ag dealership, you already know what the third week of harvest sounds like.

## The dealership on paper

Two stores 38 miles apart. Tractor and combine lines, a six-bay shop at the main store, four people across both parts counters, seven techs, three service trucks. During harvest the phone starts before the counter opens and does not stop until the last combine shuts down.

Two pressures make that phone expensive. The first is downtime. Farm Progress, writing about planter downtime, makes the basic point that delays equal lost dollars and cites research showing a 1% yield loss for every day wheat planting was delayed. Harvest windows are just as tight and often tighter. A customer who cannot reach a human at the parts counter is a customer watching money leave the field.

The second is staffing. NAEDA has said the average farm equipment dealer would hire five more technicians today. In Farm Equipment's 2026 Dealer Business Outlook survey, 63.3% of dealers said they were "most concerned" about technician availability, its second-ranked concern overall. Nobody is solving a September phone surge by hiring in September.

(For the plain distinction between an agent, a chatbot, and a phone tree, see [The Agentic Wave, Explained](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/).)

## Six rules on a legal pad

The parts manager wrote these before anyone turned anything on. They were written in English, not software:

1. Never quote a warranty outcome. Ever.
2. Never discuss an account balance, credit limit, or terms. Transfer to the office.
3. Any caller who says a machine is down in the field goes to a human inside 30 seconds, day or night.
4. Any mention of injury, fire, a shield removed, or a hydraulic leak on a running machine goes straight to the service manager's cell.
5. If the caller asks for a person twice, stop talking and transfer.
6. If cell service is bad, offer to switch to text and finish there.

Rule 6 is not a nicety. In the United Soybean Board's rural broadband study of more than 2,000 farm operators, 77% did not agree their internet provided value for the cost in their fields. A parts agent that only works on a clean voice call is useless to a guy in a cab at the far end of a section.

## 5:45 a.m. to 7:30 a.m.

Nineteen calls before the doors opened. The agent handled fourteen: hours, whether the main store had a chopper knife set in stock, three part-number lookups from serial numbers the callers read off, and two will-call holds placed under the customer's name for 7 a.m. pickup. Two callers wanted a specific parts guy by name and got voicemail. Three went to the on-call number.

## 9:40 a.m.

A customer asked whether a concave set was at store two. The agent checked both stores, found one, put a hold on it, and texted the customer a photo of the bin tag plus driving directions. Least glamorous thing it did all day, probably the most valuable. It replaced a call, a callback, and a truck driving to the wrong store.

## 11:20 a.m.

Combine down, seven miles out, rain forecast Thursday. The agent recognized "down in the field," stopped asking questions, and rang the service manager while texting him the caller's number and the machine model. Elapsed time from "she's shut down" to a human voice: 22 seconds. No triage attempted, which is the point.

## 1:15 p.m.

Three "where's my tech" calls. The agent pulled dispatch status and texted each customer an ETA window instead of reading it aloud on a bad line. Two accepted. The third wanted to argue about a travel-time charge from a prior invoice, and that went to the service writer under rule 2.

## 3:00 p.m.

A backordered header part. The agent read back the order date and supplier ETA, offered a text when the status changed, then asked whether the customer wanted the used-parts department to look for one. That question came from the parts manager's script, not from the model.

## 6:00 p.m. to 9:00 p.m.

Thirty-one after-hours contacts, mostly texts. Availability questions, four will-call holds for the morning, one post-harvest inspection booked, and two escalations: a warranty claim dispute and a caller who wanted the sales manager about a trade. Both were logged with a name, number, and one-line summary, and both got a callback by 8 a.m.

## What broke

Three things, and one of them mattered.

- The agent misheard a serial number twice on a windy call and pulled a part for the wrong model year. Caught at the counter, but that is a wasted trip if it reaches the truck.
- It read a long ETA window aloud instead of texting it, and the customer hung up mid-sentence.
- Two customers said flatly they did not want to talk to a computer during harvest. One has bought equipment there for 30 years. He got the parts manager's cell number and an apology, which was the right call.

Day total: 96 calls and 140 texts across both stores, roughly 60 closed without a person, 22 escalated, the rest messages.

## Mark up last harvest's worst day

Pull the busiest day from last fall's phone log and put one letter beside each call: C if it closed with a lookup, a price, or a hold, E if it had to reach a person, M if it was just a message. A thin C column means an agent buys you little. A fat one tells you exactly which calls to hand over first, and in what order.
