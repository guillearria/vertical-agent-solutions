If you run one to three stores selling bikes, quads, side-by-sides, and jet skis, you already know the phones in April. Everyone wants their machine back before the first warm weekend, and the counter staff spend the day saying "let me check." An AI phone and text agent (software that answers calls and texts and works from your rules) can take a share of that. Below are five things owners tend to believe about it, each with a verdict. Every number in this post is illustrative, not a benchmark.

## Belief 1: "Spring is too chaotic for a bot"

**Verdict: Wrong. Spring is where it pays off.**

Picture a two-store dealer taking 150 calls a day in April. Most of them fall into a few repeat types: "When can you get my bike in?", "Is my side-by-side done?", "Do you do tires and batteries while I wait?", "Can you de-winterize my Sea-Doo before Memorial Day?" None of those need a service writer's judgment. They need someone to answer fast and check the shop schedule.

The setup work is the slots. Give the agent job types with time blocks (a battery swap is not a top-end rebuild) and a daily cap per technician, and it books into openings the same way your best writer does. When the board is full, it says so and offers the next date. A chaotic system answered badly is a voicemail box with 40 messages in it. That's the thing the agent replaces.

## Belief 2: "Parts lookups have too many fitments for software"

**Verdict: Half true.**

A VIN tells you a lot: make, model, model year. It doesn't settle every fitment question. Sub-models, mid-year changes, and aftermarket swaps catch good parts people too. An agent that guesses at a brake pad part number will send a customer home with the wrong box.

What works is splitting the job. The agent collects the VIN, the year and model, and a photo by text if the part is unclear. It checks stock and price on common items you've approved, like oil filter kits, plugs, and batteries for the units you sell most. Anything else goes to the parts counter as a ticket with all of that already filled in. Your counter person stops writing down VINs over a bad cell connection and starts with a complete request.

## Belief 3: "Recall and warranty questions always need a human"

**Verdict: False for recall status, true for warranty decisions.**

Recall checks are a lookup, though powersports makes them messier than cars. Road motorcycles fall under NHTSA, whose free VIN tool covers brands such as Harley-Davidson, Honda, Kawasaki, Polaris, and BRP Can-Am. That tool has gaps: it shows only incomplete safety recalls, skips small manufacturers, and generally doesn't go back more than 15 years. Off-road ATVs and side-by-sides are a Consumer Product Safety Commission matter, and personal watercraft fall under the U.S. Coast Guard. The agent can follow that map, check the manufacturer's dealer portal if you connect it, and book the recall repair.

Whether a failed fuel pump is covered under warranty is a different question. That depends on the claim, the maintenance history, and what the manufacturer approves. The agent should answer "Your service writer will confirm coverage when the unit is inspected" and never "That should be covered."

## Belief 4: "It's just a car dealer's BDC with a different logo"

**Verdict: True for the first call, false after that.**

Answering a web lead fast, setting an appointment, and following up are the same jobs a car store's business development center handles. That comparison is covered in [this post on AI agents versus BDC reps for small dealerships](/blog/should-a-small-dealership-use-an-ai-agent-or-a-bdc/), so it isn't repeated here.

After that first call, a powersports sale looks different. Buyers want to sit on the bike. A family may be pricing two quads and a trailer together. Demand runs in short seasons, and inventory of a hot model can sell out in a weekend. The agent's job is to confirm a unit is on the floor, book a showroom visit, and send the lead to a salesperson with notes. If you sell watercraft, the off-season slowdown matters for what you pay, and [the marina post on annual pricing](/blog/why-a-marina-should-price-its-ai-phone-agent-by/) covers that well.

## Belief 5: "If it's polite, it can talk money and rides too"

**Verdict: False. Here are three hard lines.**

**Trade-in values.** A trade's value depends on hours, condition, modifications, and the aftermarket exhaust the owner swears is worth $800. Any number the agent says becomes the customer's anchor. The agent collects the year, model, mileage or hours, and photos, then books an appraisal.

**Payments and credit terms.** Federal Truth in Lending rules (Regulation Z) treat certain terms in credit advertising as triggers: the down payment, the number of payments or length of the loan, the payment amount, and the finance charge. If any of those appear, the ad also has to disclose things like the APR and the full repayment terms. A text that says "$189 a month" sent to a list of leads can start to look a lot like an ad. Beyond that, credit decisions belong to your finance office. Have the agent say financing is available and book time with your finance manager. Have your compliance attorney or your lender's dealer rep review the exact wording.

**Test rides and rentals.** These raise questions about license endorsements, waivers, insurance, and age limits, and those vary by state and by your insurer. The agent can take a name and the unit of interest and schedule a callback. Any call about a crash, an injury, or damage on a rental unit goes straight to a manager, with no troubleshooting first.

## Before the April phones light up

Pull last spring's call log, or have the counter tally calls for one busy week, and sort every call into five piles: service booking, status, parts, recall, sales. Write down the exact sentence the agent should say on trade values, payments, and ride requests. When you talk to vendors, ask which ones connect directly to your dealer management system and parts catalog. Then run 20 test calls in each pile before launch, including a few where the caller pushes hard for a monthly payment.
