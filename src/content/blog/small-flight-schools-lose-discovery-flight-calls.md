At a school with eight airplanes and five instructors, the person who answers the phone is often the chief instructor, sitting in the right seat at 3,000 feet. So the call goes to voicemail. Sometimes it's a renter asking whether a certain tail number is back from its 100-hour inspection. Sometimes it's someone who wants to buy a discovery flight for their dad's birthday, and when nobody picks up, they call the next school on the list.

This post compares three ways to cover that line, using five kinds of calls a small school actually gets. The three options are an AI phone and text agent, the self-serve features already built into scheduling platforms like Flight Schedule Pro, and a part-time front-desk dispatcher.

## The three options

- **AI phone and text agent.** It answers every call and text, works from your schedule and price list, and takes messages or books flights within rules you set.
- **Scheduling-platform self-service.** Flight Schedule Pro advertises self-service scheduling for enrolled students. In 2025 it added a branded discovery flight booking page with payment built in. A gift buyer can submit a request and pay, and staff get an alert to finish the booking. Its published materials don't describe a separate voucher or gift-certificate system, so ask the vendor before you count on one.
- **Part-time dispatcher.** A person at the desk during your busiest hours, usually weekday afternoons and weekends.

## Call 1: Discovery flights and gift certificates

**Software:** Strong for the buyer who has already found your website and is ready to pay. Weak for the caller who has questions first: "Can my 14-year-old do it?" "Can I take a picture of my wife in the left seat?"

**AI agent:** Strong. It can answer the questions you've written out for it (price, length, weight limits, what to wear), text the caller your booking link, and log gift purchases for staff to confirm. It also works at 9 p.m., which is when gift shoppers tend to call.

**Dispatcher:** Strong while on shift, and better than either of the others at reading an excited or nervous first-timer. But a shift covers maybe 25 hours a week.

## Call 2: Lesson reschedules

**Software:** The best of the three for students who will open the app, and most of them will.

**AI agent:** Useful for the students who call or text anyway. It can offer open slots that match the student's assigned CFI and aircraft type, inside rules the chief instructor sets, such as never moving a stage check without instructor approval.

**Dispatcher:** Handles it fine, but it's the least valuable use of a paid hour.

## Call 3: Weather and maintenance cancellations that ripple

The three options differ most on this call. A low ceiling or a grounded 172 doesn't cancel one flight. It wipes out the 8, 10, and noon blocks and leaves three students and two CFIs waiting to hear what happens next.

**AI agent:** Good at the *notification* half. Once a CFI or the chief instructor has made the call, the agent can text every affected student, offer new slots, and handle the "so am I flying or not?" replies. **It must never make the go/no-go call itself.** It should never tell a student "looks fine to me," and never read out a forecast as advice. If a student pushes back ("the METAR says 1,200 broken, why can't I go?"), the agent says the instructor made the decision and offers to have them call the student back.

**Software:** It will send cancellation notices, but someone still has to rearrange the day by hand.

**Dispatcher:** The best at rearranging the day, if they have experience. Juggling five schedules takes judgment.

## Call 4: Financing and VA-benefit questions

**AI agent:** It can state facts you give it: which lenders you work with, whether your school takes VA benefits, and where to find VA's own flight-training page. VA's rules matter here. Its flight-training benefits require an FAA-certified Part 141 or 142 school with a VA-approved program, so a Part 61-only school should say plainly that it doesn't qualify. Questions about one person's eligibility go to your school certifying official or to VA. The agent should not try to answer them.

**Software:** No help here.

**Dispatcher:** Same limits as the agent, unless they are also your certifying official.

## Call 5: Airworthiness questions and squawks

A renter calls: "The left mag drops 200 RPM, is that okay?" Or a student asks whether the annual inspection is current.

**All three:** These calls get handed off every time. Under 14 CFR 91.7, the pilot in command is responsible for deciding whether the aircraft is in condition for safe flight, and that decision depends on maintenance records and a mechanic's judgment. A bot can't supply either one, and neither can a part-time front-desk hire. The agent's job is to log the squawk (tail number, time, and the caller's description), never ground or clear an aircraft, and page the chief instructor or your mechanic right away.

The same rule covers medical certificates and FAA eligibility. "Will my ADHD medication disqualify me?" goes to a CFI who can point the caller to an aviation medical examiner. The agent's script is short: "That's a question for our chief instructor. I've sent them your number, and here's when to expect a call."

## Illustrative monthly costs

These are rough examples for planning, not quotes:

| Option | Illustrative monthly cost | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| AI phone/text agent | $100–$400, plus setup time | Every call, 24/7, within your rules |
| Scheduling-platform self-service | Often part of the plan you already pay for; Flight Schedule Pro quotes prices per aircraft | Students in the app, web buyers |
| Part-time dispatcher (~25 hrs/week) | Roughly $2,000 in wages at the BLS median receptionist rate of $18.27/hour, before payroll taxes | Shift hours only |

Most schools this size end up with a mix: software for enrolled students, an agent for after-hours calls and overflow, and people for anything that touches safety. ## Run a scrubbed-Saturday drill

Before you sign with any vendor, write out a bad Saturday: a 600-foot overcast until noon, one airplane down for a squawk, and two discovery flights on the books. Then call the agent's demo line, playing each affected person in turn. Ask it about the weather. Ask it whether the 172 is fixed. Ask it whether your medication is a problem. If it answers any of those three instead of handing them to a CFI, it isn't ready for your phone. If it sends the rescheduling texts cleanly and hands off all three, it might be.
