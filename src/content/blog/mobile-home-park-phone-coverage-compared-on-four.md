Most communities with 50 to 300 lots run their office the same way. One on-site manager works part-time, maybe 20 to 30 hours a week, and the phone rings whether or not anyone is there. Buyers asking about a lot go to voicemail. A resident with water coming up through the gravel at 11 p.m. calls the office number because it's the only one they have.

This post compares three ways to cover that phone, using the four kinds of calls that actually come in. The costs are illustrative. Your numbers will depend on call volume and your market.

## The three options

**An AI phone and text agent.** Software answers calls and texts at any hour. It reads from a script you approve, collects details, and forwards anything outside its script to a person.

**A live answering service.** Human operators at a call center take messages and follow your instructions for emergencies. They are real people, but they don't know your park.

**More hours for your on-site manager.** The person who knows every lot, every resident, and where the shutoff valves are gets more time in the office.

## Call type 1: Lot availability and homes for sale

These are your revenue calls. Someone saw a sign or a listing and wants to know what's open, what lot rent is, whether pets are allowed, and when they can look.

- **AI agent: strong.** It answers from a list you keep current (open lots, homes for sale, asking prices, lot rent, pet policy). It can text photos or a link to an application and book a showing on the manager's calendar. The risk is a stale list. If it says a home is available after it sold, you lose trust.
- **Answering service: weak.** Operators usually take a message. The buyer waits until the manager calls back, and by then may have called the park down the road.
- **Manager hours: strong, but only during office hours.** Many of these calls come in evenings and weekends, when people have time to shop.

## Call type 2: After-hours water and sewer line breaks

This is where who owns the pipe matters. In many communities the park is responsible for lines up to each lot's shutoff or service point, and the resident owns the line from there to the home. Check your own lease and state rules, because the split varies.

- **AI agent: good for triage, not for fixing.** It can ask the questions that matter: Is water pooling or spraying? Is sewage backing up? Is it near the home or out by the road? Then it calls or texts your maintenance person on a set list, keeps trying down that list until someone confirms, and tells the resident what happens next. You have to write that call list and test it at 2 a.m. before you rely on it.
- **Answering service: good.** Taking emergency calls and paging someone is a job these services have done for decades. Operators can hear panic in a voice better than software can.
- **Manager hours: poor.** More daytime hours don't cover nights, unless the manager also agrees to be on call.

## Call type 3: Lot-rent payment questions

"What's my balance?" "Did my check post?" "Where do I drop off a money order?" "Can I pay online?"

- **AI agent: good for the routine ones.** Payment methods, due dates, the drop box location, and the online portal link are easy. Balance lookups only work if the agent connects to your property software, and you should require some identity check before it reads out anyone's balance.
- **Answering service: weak.** Operators can repeat basic instructions but can't see accounts.
- **Manager hours: strong.** The manager knows who's behind and why.

## Call type 4: Rule and complaint calls

Barking dogs, cars parked on lawns, a neighbor's overgrown yard, a speeding golf cart.

- **AI agent: fair.** It can log the complaint with the lot number, time, and details, quote the written rule back, and send it to the manager as a ticket. It shouldn't promise enforcement or take sides between neighbors.
- **Answering service: fair.** They take a message. It's roughly the same result, and it costs more per call.
- **Manager hours: strong.** Neighbor disputes are relationship work, and residents want to hear from someone they know.

## Calls that always go to a person

Whatever you choose, write these into the rules as instant handoffs:

- **Rent-increase disputes.** The agent can say where a notice was sent. It should never explain, defend, or negotiate an increase. Some states set strict rules here. California, for one, requires at least 90 days' written notice of a lot-rent increase.
- **Eviction, nonpayment notices, and anything about a notice a resident received.** Timing and wording carry legal weight. One wrong sentence from software can become evidence.
- **Fair-housing-sensitive applicant questions.** Examples: "Do you allow kids?", "Is this a safe area for us?", or questions touching disability, religion, or national origin. The Fair Housing Act protects families with children. A 55-and-older community may qualify for an exemption under the Housing for Older Persons Act, but only on familial status, and only if it meets the occupancy and age-verification requirements. Every other protection still applies. Send these calls to a trained person.

The [HOA management post](/blog/setting-up-an-ai-phone-agent-for-the-homeowner/) covers a similar split between routine calls and sensitive ones, if you manage both kinds of property.

## Illustrative monthly costs

Say you run a 150-lot community that gets about 250 calls a month, averaging 2.5 minutes each. That's roughly 625 minutes.

| Option | Rough monthly cost | Covers nights? |
|---|---|---|
| AI phone and text agent | $50 to $400 in subscription fees, plus a few hours of your setup time | Yes |
| Live answering service | About $900 to $2,000 on per-minute plans (AnswerConnect lists 300 minutes for $395, with overage around $1.85 to $2.50 a minute; Ruby lists 500 minutes for $1,725) | Yes |
| 20 more manager hours a week | About $1,700 to $2,000, assuming roughly $19 to $23 an hour plus payroll taxes | No, unless on call |

Published pay for mobile home park managers varies a lot by source and region, so use your own wage figure.

## How the three stack up

| Call type | AI agent | Answering service | Manager hours |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lot and home-for-sale inquiries | Strong | Weak | Strong (daytime only) |
| After-hours water/sewer breaks | Good for triage | Good | Poor |
| Lot-rent payment questions | Good (routine questions) | Weak | Strong |
| Rules and complaints | Fair | Fair | Strong |

For many owners, the answer is a combination. The AI agent takes sales calls, payment questions, and complaint intake at any hour, and pages maintenance for breaks. The manager's existing hours go to disputes, notices, and applicants. The [200-unit apartment community post](/blog/does-an-ai-leasing-assistant-actually-work-at-a/) shows a similar split for leasing calls.

## Before you sign up for anything

Pull one month of call logs and voicemails and sort each call into the four buckets above, plus a fifth for the must-go-to-a-human topics. If most of your volume is sales and payment questions, test an AI agent. If your biggest worry is a sewer backup at 3 a.m. and sales calls are light, an answering service may be the better buy. Whichever you pick, call it yourself after dark with a fake burst-pipe report before you trust it with a real one.
