On February 13 a two-location flower shop has three people on the phones and eleven people trying to call. The callers who hear a busy signal don't try again tomorrow. They order from the next florist on Google.

That missed-call problem is why florists keep hearing about AI phone agents, meaning software that answers the call, holds a real conversation, and writes up the order. Several vendors now pitch agents built for flower shops. Dialzara, 1n1.ai, and AVA all advertise taking delivery orders, card messages, and sympathy requests. Before you trust one with your busiest week, it helps to check what florists commonly believe about them. Here are five of those beliefs, each with a verdict.

## Belief 1: "Customers won't order flowers from a bot"

**Verdict: Partly true, but mostly about bad bots.**

Callers hang up on phone trees and on voicemail. A caller who wants "a dozen red roses delivered to my wife's office Friday, around $90" is filling out a form out loud, and an agent that can follow the conversation handles that well. The Society of American Florists has found roses are the pick for most Valentine's buyers, so a large share of that week's calls ask for nearly the same thing.

What does lose customers is trapping them. Set the agent so that saying "person" or pressing 0 always reaches a human or books a callback. The people who want to talk through ideas will ask for someone, and the people who are in a hurry get their order in.

## Belief 2: "Sympathy orders are too sensitive"

**Verdict: Half right. It depends on who is calling.**

A coworker ordering a standing spray for a 10 a.m. service at a named funeral home is giving you routine details: the funeral home, the name of the person who died, the service time, a price tier, and a card message. An agent speaking slowly and plainly can take that order.

A daughter planning her father's casket spray is a different call. She needs someone who will coordinate with the funeral director and not hurry her. Write a simple rule for the agent: if the caller is immediate family, it stops taking the order and passes the call to a person. Funeral homes face the same question, and [this post on where they draw the line](/blog/would-an-ai-answer-a-grieving-familys-call-where/) is worth reading before you write your rule.

## Belief 3: "The wire service already covers it"

**Verdict: Mostly false for your own phone line, with one exception to check.**

FTD, Teleflora, and BloomNet (the 1-800-Flowers network) mainly send you orders placed through other florists and websites. They don't answer calls from your regular customers. The exception is Teleflora's 24 Hour Flowers, which puts trained floral sales consultants on your overflow calls. It lists at $49.95 a month, and that fee is waived in any month it captures five or more orders. If you're already a Teleflora member, get a quote from them and compare it with an AI vendor. Also keep in mind a warning florists give each other: take on too many wire orders in a holiday week and you can end up turning away customers who pay full price.

## Belief 4: "It will garble addresses and card messages"

**Verdict: A real risk that the right setup catches.**

Your own staff mishear things on February 13 too. The fix works the same for software and for people:

- **Card messages read back word for word,** including punctuation and the sign-off ("Love always, J"). The caller confirms, or the agent tries again.
- **Street names spelled out** and checked against your list of delivery zones before a price is quoted.
- **A text confirmation** sent to the caller with the address and card message, so the caller can catch any mistake before the order reaches your design table.

Don't sign with a vendor that can't do word-for-word read-back.

## Belief 5: "Holiday volume is too chaotic to automate"

**Verdict: Backwards for the calls, correct for the stock.**

Most of the holiday crush is the same three questions asked hundreds of times: what can I get for $75, can it arrive today, and where's my order. An agent can answer many calls at once, quote your price tiers, apply your cutoff times ("same-day delivery orders close at 1 p.m."), and look up order status. That volume is the easy part.

The harder part is running out of stock. When the red roses are gone at noon, someone has to update what the agent offers and decide on substitutions. Choosing between garden roses and a mixed arrangement for a customer who asked for red roses is a design decision for a person on your staff. We saw the same pattern in [a propane dealer's pre-freeze call surge](/blog/a-propane-dealer-put-an-ai-agent-on-the-phone-the/): the agent handled the volume well, but only because humans kept its information current.

## What stays at the counter

- Weddings, galas, and other custom event work
- Funeral coordination with a grieving family
- Substitutions when stock runs out
- Complaints about a delivered arrangement

## Test calls to run before Valentine's week

Call your own line and try to trip it up:

1. Order a mid-tier arrangement to an address with an unusual street name, and check the spelling on the ticket.
2. Dictate a card message with a comma, a nickname, and an emoji described in words.
3. Ask for same-day delivery at 1:05 p.m., five minutes after your cutoff.
4. Give an address just outside your delivery zone.
5. Say you're calling about your mother's funeral, and time how long it takes to reach a person.
6. Ask about an item you've marked sold out.
7. Ask for status on a real order number and on a made-up one.

## Pick a slow Tuesday in January

Run those seven calls in January, when a mistake costs you nothing. Then compare the order tickets with what you actually said. If the card messages and addresses come out exactly right, the agent is ready for February. If they don't, you'll find out before the calls start piling up.
