The roll-off phone rings in bursts. Contractors at 6:45 a.m., homeowners on their lunch break, and "where's my box" at 4:30 when the last driver is already loaded. Most of those calls are the same dozen questions with different ZIP codes attached. A handful of them can cost you a container, a landfill fine, or a claim on your general liability policy if anyone answers them wrong, human or machine.

This is a sorting checklist for that phone. Work through it before you turn an AI phone and text agent loose, not after.

(If you also rent skid steers and mini excavators to contractors, that side of the yard has its own list of questions worth asking first: [11 Questions Equipment Rental Yards Ask Before Handing the Phone to an AI Agent](/blog/11-questions-equipment-rental-yards-ask-before/). Different liability, different call mix.)

## The rule that decides everything below

If the answer lives in your rate sheet, your accepted-materials list, or your dispatch calendar, the agent can say it out loud.

If the answer depends on what a driver sees at the curb, what the scale ticket says, or what a city inspector decides, the agent takes notes and stops talking.

## Green light: let the agent own these end to end

- **Size and price quotes by ZIP and material.** Load the rate card the way you actually price it: box size, included tonnage, haul radius, and material type. A 20-yard box with a 3-ton allowance is a different quote than a 20-yard clean concrete load, and the agent should ask which one it is before naming a number.
- **Same-day availability and delivery windows.** Only if it reads live truck capacity. An agent promising "before noon" off a stale spreadsheet is worse than voicemail.
- **Swap-outs, empty-and-returns, and final pickup requests.** Confirm the address, the box number, and whether the lid is closed and the load is level. That last question saves a dry run.
- **Rental period extensions and daily fees.** Flat, published, unambiguous. Let the agent quote it and text the confirmation.
- **Prohibited-material screening at the top of the call.** The agent reads your banned list and asks directly: any tires, mattresses, paint, batteries, or appliances with refrigerant? Nearly every hauler bans tires because landfills won't take them, and appliances containing refrigerant need certified recovery under EPA Section 608 rules before they can be scrapped. The agent's job is to catch it on the phone, not to solve it.
- **Placement prep instructions.** Clearance length, overhead wires, gate width, plywood under the wheels.
- **After-hours capture with a next-morning callback slot.** The 9 p.m. contractor booking Monday's box is the easiest money on this list.

## Yellow: agent gathers, a human confirms

- **Heavy material loads.** Concrete, dirt, brick, and shingles hit a weight cap long before the box looks full. The agent can quote your dedicated clean-fill container and should flag anything mixed for a dispatcher callback.
- **Tricky placement.** Gravel drives, alleys, tight cul-de-sacs, new asphalt. The agent collects photos by text and lets the driver or dispatcher make the call.
- **Permit questions.** Street or right-of-way placement usually requires a municipal permit, and who pulls it (you or the customer) varies by city. The agent can say a permit is likely needed and route the call. It should never say "you're fine."
- **Commercial accounts and credit terms.** Capture the company, volume, and job type. Terms are an owner conversation.

## Red line: transfer or schedule a callback, always

- **Overweight and tonnage overage disputes.** Overage is typically billed per ton above the included allowance and moves with local tipping fees. A customer arguing about a scale ticket wants a decision on their bill. Only a person makes that.
- **Hazardous or suspected-hazardous material.** Asbestos, drums of unknown liquid, anything the customer describes vaguely.
- **Damage claims.** Cracked driveway, gouged lawn, clipped curb, broken sprinkler head. The agent's only line is to take the address, take photos, and get a human on it today. Anything resembling an admission of fault belongs to you and your insurer.
- **Code enforcement, citations, and permit violations.** If a city officer is standing next to the box, that call goes to a phone that a human answers.
- **Fires, injuries, or accidents involving a container or truck.**

## Eight test calls to make before you go live

Have someone who doesn't work for you place these, then listen to the recordings.

1. "How much for a 20-yard for a bathroom gut?" (Does it ask about material?)
2. "Can you get one out today?" (Does it check real capacity?)
3. "I've got about 4 tons of broken concrete." (Should route to the heavy-debris container.)
4. "Can I throw a fridge and two mattresses in there?" (Should refuse cleanly, not guess.)
5. "It's going in the street in front of my house." (Should raise the permit question and route.)
6. "Your driver cracked my driveway." (Should stop selling and escalate.)
7. "Why is my bill $180 higher than the quote?" (Should not attempt to explain the scale ticket.)
8. "We're a GC, we'd do 15 pulls a month, what are your terms?" (Should capture and route to you.)

If any of these ends with the agent improvising, fix the script before the number goes live. For a plain-English look at why these systems handle open-ended calls differently from an old phone tree, see [The Agentic Wave, Explained](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/).

## Pick one week and one call type

Start with after-hours quote calls only. Everything else keeps rolling to your existing line. Review twenty recordings at the end of the week, count how many quotes turned into delivered boxes, and only then open up swap-outs and extensions. If the agent gets one overweight or permit call wrong in that first week, you'll know exactly which line of the script to rewrite.
