Locksmiths have a pricing problem they didn't create. Consumer warnings from the Better Business Bureau and state regulators describe a familiar scam: someone quotes a very low price over the phone, then comes up with reasons to raise it once they're at the door. Honest shops get judged by that reputation. An AI phone agent can help with this or make it worse, depending on how plainly it talks about money.

The walkthrough below is a composite. It's one Friday night at an independent shop with four vans, put together from the kinds of calls these businesses typically get. Every price in it is made up to show the setup. None of them are market rates.

## The setup

The shop puts its after-hours fee schedule on its website: a $65 service call fee, home lockouts at $95 to $175 total, car lockouts at $85 to $150, and rekeys at $25 per cylinder plus the trip charge. The agent can say those numbers and no others. One tech is on call each night and the owner's cell is the backup. The owner wrote the escalation rules, and they're at the end of this post.

## 6:10 p.m. A rekey after a move

A woman closed on a house on Wednesday and wants the locks rekeyed. The agent asks how many exterior doors she has (three, plus the garage entry), gives her the per-cylinder price and the trip fee, and books her for Saturday at 10 a.m. It also tells her the tech will ask to see her closing paperwork or ID with the new address.

**Closed by the agent.** No human involved.

## 6:45 p.m. A car lockout in a grocery store parking lot

An adult is alone, the keys are on the seat, and the car isn't running. The agent gives the $85 to $150 range, collects the make, model, and location, and explains that the tech will need to see an ID that matches the registration before opening the car. Then it texts everything to the on-call tech.

**Handed off for verification.** The agent never dispatches a lockout by itself. The tech calls back, asks the caller to have a photo of the registration ready, and leaves.

The archived FTC consumer guide on finding a locksmith tells customers to expect a locksmith to ask for their ID. Checking ownership protects the shop too. If a tech opens a car for the wrong person, the shop has helped with a theft.

## 7:50 p.m. A key fob for a 2019 SUV

The caller lost his only fob. The agent gives the published range for programming and explains that the exact price depends on the vehicle. It asks for the VIN, which the shop uses to check the part, and books a Monday appointment as "pending part confirmation."

**Mostly closed.** The owner confirms the part on Monday morning.

## 8:30 p.m. A toddler in a locked car

A mother calls from a gas station. Her son is buckled in his car seat and the keys are locked inside. The owner treats this as an emergency. The agent's first words are an instruction to hang up and call 911. NHTSA's guidance is that anyone who sees a child alone in a car should call 911 right away, because the temperature inside a car can climb 20 degrees in as little as 10 minutes. The agent sends a text with the shop's number, pages the on-call tech, and flags the call red.

**Human only, after 911.** The agent doesn't quote a price, and it never suggests the caller wait for a van instead of calling 911.

## 9:15 p.m. A restaurant back door that won't lock

The closer arm on a commercial door has broken, and the manager can't secure the building for the night. The restaurant is an account customer already on file. The agent confirms the account, gives the commercial after-hours fee, and pages the tech, because the owner's rule says any building that can't be secured goes to a person.

## 10:05 p.m. A renter locked out

His keys are inside his apartment. The agent gives the range and explains that the tech will need to see an ID with that address or a lease, and that if neither is available, the landlord may need to be contacted. The tech takes it from there.

## 11:20 p.m. "I need my locks changed tonight"

The caller is speaking quietly. Her ex still has a key. The agent has been trained to recognize this kind of call. It doesn't ask for details or proof, and it doesn't quote a price. It asks whether she's safe right now and tells her that if she isn't, she should call 911. It offers the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788). Then it asks whether texting her phone is safe before it sends anything, because an abuser may be able to see her messages. Finally, it calls the owner's cell directly.

Some states have laws for this situation. California's Civil Code 1941.5, for example, requires a landlord to change an eligible tenant's locks within 24 hours of a documented request. The agent doesn't explain that to the caller. The owner handles that conversation.

## 12:40 a.m. The call the agent got wrong

A homeowner locked out of her house asked for a price. The agent gave the $95 to $175 range. When the tech got there, he found a high-security deadbolt that cost more than the top of the range to open. He called her before starting, as the rules require, and she agreed to the price. Her review the next day still said "quoted one price, charged another."

That review describes the scam pattern this shop is trying hard not to look like. The owner made two changes. The agent now says, "That range covers standard locks. High-security brands cost more, and the tech will confirm before touching anything." It also asks callers whether they know the brand of their lock.

## The owner's written escalation rules

- **Agent closes:** rekey bookings, published fees and ranges, fob appointments pending part checks, status questions.
- **Agent collects details, then a human verifies before dispatch:** every home, car, and business lockout.
- **911 first, then a human:** a child, a vulnerable adult, or a pet locked in a car or in a home where someone is in danger.
- **Owner's cell directly:** anyone afraid of a person who has a key, and any building that can't be secured.
- **Never:** quote anything below the published fee, promise an arrival time the tech hasn't confirmed, or argue about price.
- **Always:** give the shop's legal business name, and its license number if the caller asks. Thirteen states license locksmiths statewide, including California, Texas, and Illinois.

## Before the vans roll on a live call

Before you sign with any phone agent vendor, write your own version of the rules list above on one page. Then make ten test calls yourself: the child in the car, the whispered lock-change request, and the high-security deadbolt. If the agent can't handle all three exactly the way your rules say, it isn't ready to answer your phone.
