Most quotes you'll get for an AI phone agent are a single number: a per-minute rate, or a flat monthly plan. That number is real, but it's maybe half your actual bill. Below is the full stack for a shop running 4 to 15 techs, priced against what it replaces, with the break-even worked out in jobs rather than percentages.

If you're still fuzzy on what separates an agent from the phone tree you already have, [this explainer](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/) covers it in one read.

## Line item 1: the voice agent itself

Two pricing shapes dominate.

**Per minute.** Infrastructure-level platforms run roughly $0.05 to $0.15 a minute. Managed platforms that include CRM integration, support, and a build-out land closer to $0.25 to $0.50.

**Flat monthly.** Simpler standalone answering products publish plans in the $50 to $100 a month range, usually with caps on minutes or unique callers.

Say you route after-hours calls plus daytime overflow to the agent: about 250 calls a month at roughly three minutes each, or 750 minutes. At $0.35, that's **$260 a month**.

## Line item 2: telephony

You need a number and inbound minutes. Twilio charges $0.0085 per minute for inbound calls to a US local number, and local numbers rent for a dollar or two a month. Your 750 minutes cost about **$8**. Many managed vendors bundle this, so ask whether it's included or passed through at cost.

## Line item 3: the field service software connection

Your agent is only worth paying for if it writes the job into the calendar you actually dispatch from. Jobber publishes plans at $29, $99, $149, and $399 a month, with the middle tiers covering up to 15 users. Housecall Pro's published tiers run in the same neighborhood at the low end and a few hundred at the top. ServiceTitan doesn't publish pricing at all; it's per technician, quoted after a demo, and it now sells its own AI voice agent built directly into the platform.

You're already paying this, so it isn't new money. What can be new money: some voice vendors put two-way calendar write-back on a higher tier than the plan you were quoted. Get that in writing before you sign.

Budget: **$0 extra if your tier already includes it, $50 to $150 if it doesn't.**

## Line item 4: setup

Self-serve products charge nothing and you configure them yourself in an afternoon. Done-for-you builds (custom scripts, service area rules, membership lookup, tech skill routing) get quoted in the hundreds to low thousands as a one-time fee. Amortized over a year, a $1,200 build adds **$100 a month**.

**All in for a 9-tech shop: roughly $370 to $520 a month.**

## What comes off the other side of the ledger

**The answering service.** US live services commonly bill $1 to $2 a minute or $1.50 to $3 per answered call, and contractor bills often land between $150 and $500 a month, higher for real 24/7 coverage. In a peak month with 400 after-hours calls at three minutes and $1.50 a minute, that's $1,800.

**Dispatcher overtime.** A CSR at $22 an hour costs $33 in overtime. Ten extra hours a week through a four-week cold snap is $1,320.

**The calls nobody answered.** This is the big one, and it's the number you can pull yourself. Ask your phone system for missed and abandoned inbound calls between 5 p.m. and 8 a.m., and for July and January specifically.

## The heat wave week, in arithmetic

Normal volume: 12 calls a day. Five-day heat wave: it triples to 36. That's 120 extra calls in a week. Your two CSRs absorb maybe 70 of them. Fifty roll to voicemail.

Homeowners with no cooling do not leave voicemails and wait. They call the next contractor. If just one in five of those fifty would have booked, that's 10 jobs. HVAC repair invoices commonly run $250 to $900, with averages usually cited around $450 to $650. Ten jobs at $450 is $4,500 in gross revenue from one week, against a monthly agent cost under $520.

**Break-even:** at $450 a month all in and roughly $200 of gross margin on a $450 repair, you need **two to three captured jobs a month.** One of those calls turning into a system replacement, where full installs commonly run $7,500 to $15,500, covers the agent for a year or more. A landscaping company facing the same spring surge math [ran into the same shape of problem](/blog/can-an-ai-answering-service-keep-up-with-a/).

## Three things it should not be doing

**Quoting a replacement.** A system swap is a five-figure decision involving load calculations, ductwork, and rebate eligibility. The agent captures interest and books a comfort advisor. Nothing more.

**Handling a gas smell.** Utilities are unanimous: leave the building, don't use phones or switches inside, call 911 and the gas emergency line from a safe distance. Your agent's only correct behavior is to say that immediately and end the call. Do not let it collect an address first.

**Promising an arrival window during a surge.** In a heat wave, "we'll have someone out this afternoon" is the fastest way to turn a captured call into a bad review. Confirm the booking, be honest about the queue.

## Get your own number before you get a quote

Pull one report: missed inbound calls after hours for your worst month last year. Multiply by your average repair invoice, then by a conservative booking rate of 20%. If that number is under $500, you don't need this yet. If it's over $2,000, the pricing above is a rounding error, and the towing company's [three-way after-hours comparison](/blog/the-overnight-phone-at-a-6-truck-towing-company/) is worth reading before you pick a vendor.
