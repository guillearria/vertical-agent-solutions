The moment a TPA pushes an assignment to your queue, a timer starts. Programs vary, but restoration networks commonly expect first contact with the insured inside 15 to 30 minutes and a truck on site inside two to four hours, and your scorecard records every miss. Contractors who hold response times and satisfaction scores near the top of the network get noticeably more assignments than average performers. Nobody sends you a warning email when you slip. The assignment volume just quietly thins out.

So the after-hours phone is not a customer service question at a restoration firm. It is a program compliance question with a revenue tail.

## Two call types, two different clocks

Lump these together and every comparison below gets muddy.

**Emergency mitigation intake.** A supply line let go, a sprinkler head popped, sewage is backing into a finished basement. Minutes matter, the facts gathered on that call determine what truck and what crew roll, and the timestamp goes on a compliance record.

**Reconstruction-phase status calls.** "When is my drywall going up?" "Did the flooring get ordered?" These come from homeowners four weeks into a rebuild, they arrive all day and some evenings, and they are emotionally loaded but almost never urgent. They need accurate schedule information, not speed.

A setup that is excellent at one can be mediocre at the other. Price them separately.

## What a usable first notice of loss actually contains

Before judging the three options, write down what a dispatchable intake looks like at your shop. Most include:

- Caller name, callback number, and whether they are owner, tenant, or property manager
- Property address and whether anyone is on site now
- Loss type: water, fire and smoke, sewage, mold
- Source, and whether the water has been shut off
- Enough source detail to hint at contamination (clean supply line versus toilet overflow versus ground water)
- Affected floors and rooms, standing water, power on or off, occupancy, pets
- Access: gate code, lockbox, parking, who is meeting the crew
- Carrier, claim number if one exists, TPA program, or self-pay

One line to protect: nobody on the phone assigns an IICRC S500 water category. Category 1, 2, and 3 are determinations a certified tech makes on site, and clean water degrades toward Category 2 within roughly 48 to 72 hours anyway. Intake collects source facts. The field makes the call.

## The three options, side by side

| | AI phone-and-text agent | Restoration answering service | In-house coordinator + on-call |
|---|---|---|---|
| Answers in | Under two rings, always | 20 seconds to several minutes, worse in surge | Depends who is awake |
| Simultaneous calls | Effectively unlimited | Limited by staffed pool | One |
| FNOL completeness | Follows your full script every time | Good if restoration-trained, thinner if generic | Best judgment, worst consistency |
| Documentation trail | Timestamped transcript, recording, structured fields into DASH or Albi | Message log, often narrative text | Whatever gets typed up later |
| Cost at 60 after-hours calls/month | Roughly $250 to $600 all in | Roughly $400 to $900, more in surge | $70k+ loaded, plus stipends, still not 24/7 |
| Weakest point | Confidently books work you have no capacity for | Cost and hold times spike exactly when you're busiest | Human single point of failure |

Cost notes, all illustrative. Restoration-specialty live answering runs about $1.50 to $2.50 per minute, so 60 calls at four minutes lands in the mid hundreds before base fees and overage. AI voice all-in costs commonly run $0.12 to $0.25 per minute in real deployments, which makes the same volume a rounding error; the real spend is the platform subscription, the integration into your job software, and the setup work. A restoration coordinator averages around $56,000 nationally before payroll load, and one coordinator covers roughly a third of a week.

## The multi-loss storm night

Twenty-two calls in forty minutes is where these separate.

The **answering service** queues. Callers who waited eight minutes on hold have already dialed the franchise down the road, and your invoice that month has an overage line you did not budget.

The **coordinator** takes call one while calls two through six go to voicemail. Good notes on one job, nothing on five.

The **AI agent** picks up all twenty-two and produces twenty-two complete intakes, which is exactly the problem if you own six air movers and two techs. Its failure mode is not dropping calls; it is promising response you cannot deliver. Build the governor in before launch: after a set number of open emergencies, the agent stops giving arrival windows, says a coordinator will confirm within the hour, and pages the on-call manager. The same triage discipline shows up in [how roll-off dumpster companies sort call types before launch](/blog/ai-phone-agents-at-roll-off-dumpster-companies/).

## Where reconstruction calls change the math

Status calls are the quiet argument for the AI agent, because they are the volume nobody staffs for. An agent wired into your job management system can answer "your cabinets are scheduled for the 14th" from live data at 7 p.m. An answering service can only take a message. A coordinator can answer well, and spends two hours a day doing it.

Draw the line at anything involving money or scope: deductibles, supplement disputes, change orders, and any conversation where a homeowner is upset about delay. Those route to a human, same day.

## Most shops end up with a hybrid

The common landing spot for a 5 to 40 person firm: AI agent answers everything first and handles complete FNOL capture and status lookups, with a warm transfer to the on-call manager for anything the script flags. The answering service becomes the backup path, not the front door. The coordinator stops being a switchboard and starts working the carrier documentation that actually protects your program standing.

## Pull last month's after-hours recordings

Take every call that came in after 6 p.m. for one month. Mark each one as emergency or status, then check which ones captured all nine intake fields above and how many minutes passed before someone called the insured back. That sheet, not a vendor demo, tells you which of the three options you are actually shopping for.
