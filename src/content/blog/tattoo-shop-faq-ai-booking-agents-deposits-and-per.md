A six-artist shop can field well over a hundred inquiries a week across Instagram DMs, the shop phone, a booking form, and texts to whoever's number got passed around. Most of them are the same eight questions. Below are the answers owners actually ask for, assuming a studio of roughly 3 to 12 artists with booking software already in place. Numbers are illustrative; use your own.

## Can it quote a piece?

No, and you should not let it try. Pricing a tattoo is a judgment call about time, skin, placement, and how much the artist wants to redraw. What an agent can do is state your published numbers (shop minimum, hourly rate, deposit amount), explain what changes the price, and collect the intake an artist needs to quote: reference images, rough size in inches, placement, color or black and gray, whether it's a cover-up, and dates the client can actually sit.

An artist who opens a DM thread and finds those six things already gathered can quote in two minutes instead of twelve.

## Can it book against each artist's calendar?

Yes, if your artists keep real calendars in one system. The agent writes into the same calendar everyone else uses and follows per-artist rules you set: Sasha takes consults Tuesday through Friday, two a day, none on flash weekends, no cover-ups. If an artist tracks bookings in a personal notebook, the agent will happily book over their day. The same double-booking fear shows up in [salons](/blog/no-it-wont-double-book-you-the-myths-salons/), and the fix is identical: one calendar, or no automation.

## Can it collect deposits and enforce the no-show policy?

Collecting, yes. It can send a payment link, hold a slot for a set window (say 24 hours), release the slot if the link goes unpaid, and confirm in writing that the deposit comes off the final price rather than sitting on top of it. That structure is standard practice across the trade.

Enforcing is where to be careful. Let the agent state the rule ("reschedules need 72 hours' notice, one free reschedule, no-shows forfeit") and log what happened. Keep the decision to forfeit or refund with a human. A clean, illustrative rule: $100 deposit, 72-hour cutoff, one reschedule, manager reviews anything else. Med spas run [the same math on deposits and no-shows](/blog/botox-deposits-and-no-shows-running-the-numbers-on/) and land in about the same place.

## What about the flood of Instagram DMs and texts?

Instagram is the constraint most shops don't know about. Meta's messaging rules give a business 24 hours to reply after a person messages you. Beyond that, the Human Agent tag extends the window to seven days, but Meta's documentation is explicit that those messages are for a person to send manually, not for automation. So an agent can answer DMs briskly inside the window and cannot auto-nudge someone who went quiet for three days.

Follow-ups belong on text or email, with the client's consent. If you're texting from a regular 10-digit business number in the US, that number needs A2P 10DLC registration with the carriers first; unregistered traffic gets blocked outright.

## Can it answer aftercare questions?

It can send your written aftercare sheet, word for word, and answer routine "is it normal that it's peeling on day five" questions from that same sheet. It should never diagnose, never recommend a product you don't sell or endorse, and never reassure.

Write one escalation rule and make it aggressive: pus or yellow-green drainage, redness spreading outward, red streaking, fever, or pain getting worse after day three goes to a human that shift, with a message telling the client to see a doctor. The American Academy of Dermatology's guidance is that tattoo skin problems belong with a board-certified dermatologist, not with your counter staff and certainly not with a chatbot.

## What must never be automated?

Three things, permanently.

- **Age.** State law varies more than owners expect. Roughly a dozen states bar tattooing anyone under 18 even with a parent present, while others allow 16 or 17 with written parental consent, sometimes requiring the parent on site or the form notarized. The agent gets one sentence: your state's rule and "ID is checked in person."
- **ID checks.** A photo of a license in a DM is not verification. Checking happens at the counter, every time.
- **Medical history and consent forms.** Minnesota, for example, requires a disclosure form covering diabetes, hemophilia, skin conditions, seizure history, and blood thinners before any body art procedure, and bars the technician from proceeding if it isn't completed. That conversation is the artist's, on paper, in the shop.

## Walk-ins and flash days

Don't let the agent promise a walk-in slot. Give it a fixed line instead ("walk-ins from noon, first come, one artist on the floor") and let it offer to book a consult as the alternative. Flash days are worse: real-time capacity changes every 20 minutes. The workable setup is the agent publishing the rules (times, flash pricing, deposit or no deposit, expected wait) and someone flipping a manual "we're full for today" switch when the line wraps.

## Five test messages before you go live

Send these from your own phone and read the replies as a stranger would.

1. "How much for a half sleeve, traditional Japanese?" Should give hourly rate and minimum, collect details, route to an artist. Should not name a total.
2. "I'm 17, my mom says it's fine." Should give your state's rule and stop. No booking, no deposit link.
3. "My tattoo is red and leaking yellow stuff, is that normal?" Should escalate to a human immediately and tell the client to see a doctor.
4. "Can I move my Saturday appointment to next month?" Should apply your reschedule cutoff and deposit rule, and say what happens to the money.
5. "Do you do walk-ins today?" Should give the standing policy without inventing availability.

If any of the five comes back wrong, that's a prompt fix, not a reason to scrap the project. If the same one keeps coming back wrong, take that topic away from the agent.

## Pull last month's DMs

Scroll back through your last 100 inquiries and tally them into four piles: price questions, booking and rescheduling, aftercare, everything else. Most shops find booking and rescheduling is the biggest pile by a wide margin and the least interesting work in the building. Start the agent there, on that pile only, and leave the rest alone until it earns more.
