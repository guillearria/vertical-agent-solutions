Most calls to an RV park fall into five groups: Do you have a site open? Will my rig fit? Can I bring my dogs? When is check-in? What's the monthly rate? A good AI phone agent can answer all five at 9 p.m. on a Sunday. It can also hurt you if it tells a 42-foot fifth wheel that a 35-foot back-in site will work. This guide covers setup in the order you should do it.

## Step 1: Pull a month of calls and sort them

Before you talk to any vendor, go through your call log or voicemail for one busy month and tally what people asked. For a park with 50 to 300 sites, you'll usually see availability and rates at the top, then rig fit, pets, check-in and check-out times, and monthly or seasonal questions. Mark the calls that needed judgment, like refund requests and complaints. That second pile shows you what the agent should never handle alone.

## Step 2: Build a site sheet the agent can actually read

The agent is like a new front-desk hire on day one. It knows only what you write down. For every site, record:

- Maximum rig length, measured bumper to bumper
- Pull-through or back-in
- 30-amp, 50-amp, or both
- Full hookups, or water and electric only
- Slide-out clearance on each side, and any trees or posts in the way
- Pet rules for that area (some parks keep a pet-free loop)

Then give the agent one firm rule: if the caller's rig is within two feet of a site's limit, or they aren't sure of their length, it offers a larger site or passes the call to staff. It never guesses. A bad fit means a guest who arrives after dark and can't get into the site, and that costs you more than a lost booking.

The agent also needs your amp policy written out. If a caller has a 50-amp coach and only 30-amp sites are open, the agent should say so plainly. Whether an adapter will work is the guest's call, not the agent's.

## Step 3: Connect it to your reservation software

Your options depend on the software you already run.

- **Campspot.** Insider Perks sells an AI phone agent with a live Campspot connection. It checks real availability, describes sites, and texts the caller a secure payment link, so card numbers are never read aloud. At launch, pricing was $0.75 per minute on top of a CampVantage subscription starting at $99 a month.
- **Newbook.** Newbook says it offers an open API (a door other software can use to read and write bookings) and more than 100 integrations. Ask any voice vendor whether it has a working Newbook connection already, not one it plans to build.
- **Firefly.** Firefly lists its technology partners on its own integrations page, and at least one software review site says it has no public API. Check with Firefly before you sign anything. If there's no live connection, the agent can take the booking request, and staff confirms it the next morning.

Without a live connection, the agent should never say "you're booked." It should say "I've sent your request, and you'll get a confirmation by 10 a.m."

## Step 4: Script the money policies word for word

Paste in your deposit, cancellation, and early-departure policies exactly as they appear on your website. The agent reads the policy. It doesn't bend it. Anything that sounds like a hardship ("my husband's in the hospital, can I get my deposit back?") goes to a manager with a callback promised.

## Step 5: Plan the late-arrival call

The 11 p.m. call from someone on the highway is where an agent pays for itself, and it's also where security matters. Only callers who can give a confirmation number and the name on the booking get after-hours instructions: which site, where the map is, which lane to use. If your park uses gate codes, send them by text through the reservation system rather than reading them aloud to anyone who asks. Callers with no reservation hear what's open for tomorrow. If you're also weighing whether a live answering service should handle nights instead, [this comparison for small B&Bs](/blog/who-answers-your-b-bs-phone-at-2-a-m-three-options/) walks through the same tradeoff.

## Step 6: Set hard escalation rules

Write these as rules with no exceptions:

- **Emergencies.** Fire, a medical problem, a downed power line, a gas smell, a lost child. The agent tells the caller to hang up and dial 911, then pages your on-call person right away.
- **Long-term residents.** Complaints about neighbors, rent disputes, or eviction questions go straight to the manager. Monthly guests are closer to tenants than tourists, and a bot answering their disputes erodes trust fast. The [mobile home park comparison](/blog/mobile-home-park-phone-coverage-compared-on-four/) covers the resident side in more detail.
- **Weather closures.** The agent never decides whether the park is open, closed, or evacuating. It reads only a status message that a person has posted and dated. If there's no current message, it says a staff member will call back.

## Five test calls to run before launch

Have someone who didn't set up the agent place these calls, and listen to the recordings:

1. "I've got a 41-foot Class A with two slide-outs and 50-amp. Anything pull-through for Friday and Saturday?"
2. "I'm running late and won't be there until midnight. Where do I go?" Make this call once with a valid confirmation number and once without.
3. "I have three dogs, one's a pit mix. Is that okay?"
4. "My neighbor in the seasonal section keeps running his generator at night. What are you going to do about it?"
5. "There's a tornado warning. Should we leave?"

A pass means calls 1 through 3 get accurate answers from your real data, and calls 4 and 5 reach a human (or 911 guidance) within the first minute.

## Before the spring booking rush

This week, print your site list and fill in the maximum-length and slide-out columns for every site, measured with a tape rather than taken from memory. That sheet is the hardest part of the whole setup, and you'll need it whichever vendor you choose.
