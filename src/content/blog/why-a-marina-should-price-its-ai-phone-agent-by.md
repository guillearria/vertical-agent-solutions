Two weeks in April decide how the phone feels at a marina. Everybody wants their boat in the water by Memorial Day, the service writer is on hold with a parts supplier, and the desk phone rings with the same four questions: *Is my boat ready? What's the wait for a slip? Can you fit me in before the holiday? Do you have room for us tonight, we're coming up the coast.*

Then January arrives and the phone barely rings at all. That swing is the whole budgeting problem with an AI phone agent at a marina, boatyard, or boat dealer service department. Price it by the month and it looks either cheap or absurd, depending which month you pick. Price it by the year and the math gets honest.

## Four line items, not one

Vendors quote you a single number. There are usually four.

- **The agent itself.** Packaged AI receptionist plans run roughly $29 to $500 a month, with most small businesses landing somewhere between $49 and $299 depending on included minutes. If you build on a developer platform instead, all-in per-minute costs commonly land in the $0.10 to $0.25 range once you count the speech engine, the language model, and the platform fee. Watch the overage rate more than the headline price: a $29 plan with $0.48-a-minute overage can cost you more in May than a flat $149 plan.
- **Telephony.** Cheap, and often forgotten. A US local number on Twilio runs about $1.15 a month, inbound minutes about $0.0085, toll-free inbound closer to $0.022 a minute. At a mid-size yard this is a rounding error.
- **Setup and training.** One-time fees of $0 to about $500 are typical. This is where you load your haul-out policy, winterization packages, dockage rates, and the names of the two people who get transferred calls.
- **Integration.** The real variable. If your yard runs DockMaster or Lightspeed DMS on the service side, or Molo or Dockwa on the slip and transient side, ask whether the agent can read work-order status and write a callback note back into the system, or whether it just emails your writer. Get that quoted separately and in writing.

## What a season actually looks like on the bill

Assume a 180-slip yard with two service writers. Call volume is not flat: April, May, September, and October carry it, July and August hum along, January and February are nearly dead.

An illustration, with your own call log replacing my numbers: 700 calls in April at about 2.5 minutes each is 1,750 minutes, or roughly $260 at $0.15 a minute all-in. Four months like that plus eight quieter ones might total 6,000 to 8,000 minutes for the year. Minutes: $900 to $1,200. Telephony: under $150. Setup: a few hundred, once.

Call it **$2,000 to $6,000 a year** for a yard this size, with the top of the range assuming a packaged monthly plan plus custom integration work. That is the number to negotiate against.

One seasonal tactic: on a flat monthly plan you pay full freight in January for a phone that rings twice a day. Ask before you sign whether you can drop tiers or pause from December through February. Per-minute billing does this automatically.

## Break-even in hours and hulls

Skip percentages. Two units matter at a boatyard.

Marine service labor commonly runs $75 to $150 an hour, with dealer shops in the Northeast and South Florida at the top of that band. Winterization quotes vary widely by boat: figure a few hundred dollars for a 20-foot outboard and $800 to $1,200 for a 30-foot sailboat, with shrink wrap alone typically billed at $12 to $25 a foot.

At a $5,000 annual cost and a $110 shop rate, break-even is about **45 billed hours a year**, under one hour a week. In winterization terms it is roughly **twelve $400 contracts**, one a month, or about a day and a half of fall booking.

You clear that if the agent does three things: picks up spring rush calls that currently roll to voicemail, gives status updates on boats already in the shop so your writer stops narrating the same work order six times, and captures fall winterization sign-ups before the customer calls the yard across the harbor. A missed after-hours transient dockage call is lost revenue on a slip that was going to sit empty overnight.

## Where the agent has to hand off the phone

Be blunt about this in your script, because the failure modes are expensive.

- **Water coming in, fire, smoke, a boat dragging anchor.** Immediate transfer, no questions, no hold music.
- **Haul-out scheduling.** It depends on travel-lift availability, tide, crew, and Thursday's forecast. The agent takes the request and the boat's specs. A human sets the date.
- **Warranty claim disputes.** Engine manufacturer coverage arguments are a negotiation, not a lookup.
- **Damage and insurance conversations.** Anything with a surveyor or a claim number goes to an owner or manager.
- **Price disputes on a finished invoice.** The agent quotes published rates only, never adjusts one.

## Your move before haul-out season

Pull last October's and last April's phone records and count two things: total calls and how many went unanswered. Multiply the unanswered ones by your average repair order. If that number beats 45 billed hours, collect quotes now, while the yard is still busy enough to remember why you wanted this.
