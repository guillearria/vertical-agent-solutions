Operators of senior living communities tend to hear "AI phone agent" and picture a daughter in crisis getting a robot. That fear deserves a straight answer.

This post is about facility-based senior living: independent living, assisted living, and memory care, roughly 20 to 120 units, with one or two people covering the front desk, tour inquiries, and everything else. In-home care agencies have a different problem (scheduling churn), covered in [40 Caregivers, 60 Clients, One Bad Monday](/blog/40-caregivers-60-clients-one-bad-monday-where-a/). If you want the difference between an agent and the chatbot sitting on your website, [start here](/blog/the-agentic-wave-is-not-just-for-tech/).

Five beliefs, five verdicts.

## "A family choosing memory care will never talk to software"

**Verdict: partly true, and aimed at the wrong call.**

Nobody picks a community because the phone assistant was pleasant. But the first call is almost never the decision. It's usually three questions: do you have a studio open, what does it start at, can we tour Saturday.

Your competition on that call is not your sales director. It's voicemail. Enquire Solutions' benchmark data, drawn from more than 1.3 million inquiry and referral records across 2,700-plus senior housing and skilled nursing communities, found roughly 19% of incoming calls go unanswered daily. The uncomfortable part: missed calls happened about as often during fully staffed daytime hours as in the evening. That study is several years old now, but nothing about front-desk staffing since has made it easier.

Referral networks make the cost concrete. A lead goes out to several communities at once, and marketers who work those leads consistently say the fifth community to call back rarely reaches the family at all.

## "It will fumble an emergency"

**Verdict: correct. This one is not recycled fear.**

A resident's spouse calling at 11 p.m., a daughter calling because her mother fell, a hospice nurse calling about a discharge: none of that belongs to an agent. Ever.

Practical guardrails:

- The agent never triages, never assesses, never takes a message on anything clinical.
- Urgency cues (fall, hospital, ambulance, hospice, "is she okay") trigger an immediate transfer to the on-call manager or nurse line.
- Any caller who asks for a person gets one on the first ask, not the third.
- Before go-live, test the transfer when the line is busy, at 2 a.m., and on a holiday.

If you cannot guarantee a human answers that transfer around the clock, keep the agent off your main line entirely. Put it on the sales and inquiry number only.

## "It will say something that violates HIPAA or our license"

**Verdict: legitimate, and the risk is broader than HIPAA.**

Whether HIPAA applies to you depends on your operations. Law firms advising the sector, including McGuireWoods, note that many senior living operators handle electronic protected health information and land inside the rules, while purely residential communities that never bill electronically often sit outside them. Ask your counsel rather than assuming.

State licensing is the bigger exposure. Minnesota's UDALSA form, Texas Form 3647, Washington's disclosure of services under RCW 18.20.300, and Virginia's disclosure regulation all govern what you tell prospective residents about what you provide, and marketing memory care can trigger extra requirements. An agent that improvises "yes, we can absolutely manage that" is making a regulated claim.

So: no discussion of any named resident, including confirming that someone lives there. No opinion on whether you can handle a condition. No care-level promises. It reads approved language and offers to send the disclosure packet.

On AI disclosure itself, Utah's SB 226 (effective May 7, 2025) requires disclosing generative AI use in high-risk interactions in regulated occupations, and California's SB 243 targets companion chatbots while excluding customer-service bots. The simple policy that satisfies both: the agent says it's an automated assistant in its first sentence, every call.

## "It can't handle availability or pricing"

**Verdict: half wrong. Availability yes, quotes no.**

"Do you have anything open?" is a lookup, as long as unit availability lives somewhere the agent can read and your sales director actually keeps current. NIC MAP put senior housing occupancy at 89.9% in the second quarter of 2026, with assisted living averaging 87.9% earlier in the year, so a 90-unit community typically has a handful of units to fill and every inquiry counts.

Pricing is different, because your real number depends on a care assessment, community fee, second-person fee, and level-of-care add-ons. CareScout's 2025 survey put the national median assisted living cost at about $6,200 a month, but that median tells a specific family nothing. Script it: give the published starting range, explain that final cost follows an assessment, book the tour.

## "It costs more than a part-time receptionist"

**Verdict: wrong on the math, right to audit the vendor.**

BLS put the median receptionist wage at $17.90 an hour in May 2024. Twenty hours a week is roughly $18,600 a year before payroll taxes, and that person still isn't there evenings or Sundays. Vendor roundups put flat AI receptionist tiers in the low hundreds per month and per-minute rates between about $0.10 and $0.50, though those figures come from sellers and deserve a skeptical read.

The comparison that matters is move-ins. One additional resident at the national median is roughly $74,000 in annual revenue. Watch the setup fees, per-minute overages, and the staff hours it takes to keep scripts current.

## Running a fair test

Pull the last 30 days from your phone system. Count calls that went unanswered or to voicemail after 5 p.m. and on weekends, and separate inquiry calls from family, vendor, and staff calls. If the inquiry number is small, this isn't your bottleneck. If it's dozens, run an agent on the inquiry line only for 60 days, with an instant transfer path, and measure one thing: tours booked from calls you previously would have missed.
