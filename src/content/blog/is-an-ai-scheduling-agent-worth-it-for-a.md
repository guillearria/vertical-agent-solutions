Every figure below is **illustrative**. It's a worked example for a made-up four-appraiser residential firm, so it isn't a quote, and you should swap in your own numbers before you decide anything. The firm in the example finishes about 120 orders a month. Each order brings roughly five phone calls or texts: setting up property access with the homeowner, tenant, or listing agent, confirming the inspection window, handling a reschedule, and answering one or two "where's my report?" checks from the AMC or lender. That works out to about 600 contacts a month, and nearly all of them are routine.

## The stack, priced one line at a time

**1. Voice minutes.** Most of these calls are short. Assume 300 phone calls a month that average 2.5 minutes, for 750 minutes total. Third-party pricing roundups for build-it-yourself voice platforms put the all-in cost (voice, language model, and phone line together) somewhere around $0.13 to $0.31 per minute for Retell AI and about $0.13 to $0.20 or more for Synthflow. At 750 minutes that's roughly **$100–$230/month**.

**2. A packaged product, if you'd rather not build.** Most small firms won't put together a raw voice platform on their own. They'll pay a vendor that sells a finished "AI receptionist" with a dashboard. In this example that subscription is **$150–$400/month**. This number is a planning assumption, not a market survey, so get real quotes.

**3. Business texting.** Homeowners and tenants tend to answer texts faster than calls. Business texting in the US requires 10DLC registration. Twilio lists a one-time $46 brand fee, a $15 campaign vetting fee, and $10/month for a standard campaign. Per-message carrier charges come on top of that. Illustrative total: **$25–$40/month** after setup.

**4. Setup.** The agent needs your coverage area, each appraiser's inspection days, your rules for vacant versus occupied properties, and a link to the calendar where you track orders. Paying a freelancer or the vendor's onboarding team for that might cost **$500–$2,000 one time**. Doing it yourself takes something like 15–20 hours.

**5. Oversight.** Someone in the office reads the flagged transcripts every week. Budget **3 hours/month**.

**Illustrative monthly total: about $350–$650**, plus the one-time setup.

## Break-even, worked two ways

**In orders recovered.** Assume the firm keeps about **$450** from a typical AMC order after the AMC's cut. That figure is illustrative too, since fees vary widely by region and client. At a $500/month cost, the agent pays for itself by saving **a little over one order a month**. An order can be lost to a missed AMC acceptance window, a reassignment after nobody returned the tenant's call for two days, or an order turned down because the office was buried in phone tag. Most firms can name one of those from last month without checking.

**In appraiser hours freed.** Say the agent fully handles 60% of the 600 contacts, and each one would have cost an appraiser or office manager five minutes once you count callbacks and voicemail. That's about **30 hours a month**. Those hours only count as savings if they become something real:

- **Extra inspections.** At a few hours per order, 30 hours could mean several more completed reports.
- **A scheduler you don't have to hire.** A part-time coordinator at 20 hours a week and $20 an hour runs about $1,700 a month in wages alone.
- **Evenings back.** Worth something, but it doesn't pay the invoice.

## How the math moves with firm size

- **Solo appraiser, about 30 orders.** Usage-based costs drop to almost nothing, but the subscription and setup stay the same. Break-even is still about one order a month, and that's a bigger share of your volume. A pay-per-minute setup suits you better than a flat monthly plan.
- **Four appraisers.** This is the example above, and it's where the numbers are clearest.
- **Eight appraisers.** You probably already have a scheduler. Here the agent covers overflow, lunch hours, and texts that come in after 6 p.m. The value is that your scheduler stops spending the day on status checks.

The home inspection version of this math is in [AI Scheduling Agent Costs for a Home Inspection Business, From One Inspector to Eight](/blog/ai-scheduling-agent-costs-for-a-home-inspection/). The access logistics look similar, but inspectors don't operate under the independence rules below.

## Where the agent must stop

These rules aren't about style. They come from federal law and the guidelines your lenders follow, and they belong in the agent's instructions and in your test calls.

**It never talks about value.** That includes "Is it looking good?" and "Did it come in?" The USPAP Ethics Rule bars sharing assignment results with anyone other than the client or parties the client has authorized. A homeowner or listing agent who asks about the report gets told to contact their lender. The agent only gives order status to a verified client contact (the AMC or lender), checked against the order number.

**It doesn't accept "we need it at X."** Regulation Z (12 CFR 1026.42) bars coercing an appraiser into basing value on anything other than independent judgment. The official commentary says that rule covers creditors, mortgage brokers, AMCs, and real estate agents. Fannie Mae's Appraiser Independence Requirements also count commission-paid loan officers and real estate agents as restricted parties. When a caller names a target number or pressures the agent, it doesn't argue or promise anything. It ends the topic, saves the exact wording, and flags the call to the appraiser. That record helps you if the pressure keeps coming.

**Reconsideration-of-value requests go to the appraiser.** Since October 31, 2024, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have required lenders to run a formal borrower ROV process: one borrower-initiated request per appraisal, submitted through the lender. A borrower who calls your office upset about the number is the appraiser's call to handle, and usually that means pointing them to the lender's process. The agent's job is to take a message, not to give an opinion.

**Complaints go to a person.** That covers a tenant upset about the inspection, an agent unhappy with turnaround, or a threat to file a board complaint. The agent logs it and hands it off the same day.

A factual message is fine: "The owners redid the kitchen in 2023 and want the appraiser to know." The agent forwards it with no comment. Mortgage offices have a version of this problem from the lender side, covered in [Sorting Which Loan-Inquiry Tasks a Mortgage Brokerage Can Hand an AI Agent](/blog/sorting-which-loan-inquiry-tasks-a-mortgage/).

## Before You Price Anything, Count a Week of Calls

For five business days, have the office keep a tally sheet. Note each call and text by type (access, confirmation, reschedule, status check, value-related) and roughly how long it took. Then look back at the last quarter and count orders that were declined, reassigned, or late because of scheduling. Those two numbers will tell you whether your break-even is half an order or five. Take them to two or three AI receptionist vendors, and ask each one to show you, on a live test call, what its agent says when a loan officer asks it to "help get this to $410,000."
